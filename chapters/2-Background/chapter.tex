\chapter{Background}

In this thesis, we will consider several rheological constitutive models which explore a range of soft matter systems. In order to explore these models, we will consider a range of deformation protocols which are rheologically reinvent to each model and the material they represent. The overarching theme of this thesis and the main focus of each chapter is that we analyse the yielding (or fracture) behaviour of the material, and hopefully provide a small step to understand the origins of these behaviours. 

In this chapter, we will discuss the underlying theories of these models and provide further context and definitions for the phenomena that we will explore. We will start by introducing some basic rheology concepts which appear throughout this thesis. We secondly approach the problem of viscoelastic flows from a Newtonian perspective by introducing the generalised continuum equations for incompressible fluid flow, which include arbitrary stresses, and derive an approach for solving them for the case of Stokesian (i.e., zero Re) hydrodynamics. The bulk of the work done later in this thesis explores these concepts from a Hookean perspective, so we will introduce a model for semi-flexible filaments, exploring how non-linearity manifests in these systems and how we introduce plasticity in these models. Throughout this discussion, we will introduce some fundamental numerical techniques used in this thesis to perform the studies undertaken, as they become relevant.

\section{Introduction to Rheology} \label{sec:IntroRheology}

Rheology is the study of the response of a material to an applied flow deformation/stress  \cite{larson_structure_1999,chenRheologySoftMaterials2010,tannerEngineeringRheology2000,barnes_introduction_1989}. The term originates from the early 20th century and comes from the combination of the Greek words \quotes{rheo}, meaning \quotes{to flow} and logos, meaning to \quotes{study} \cite{barnes_introduction_1989}. While very early work by Hooke \cite{hooke_lectures_1678} and Newton \cite{newton_philosophiae_1687} laid the foundation of simple linear rheological cases, stating respectively \quotes{the power of any spring is in the same proportion with the tension thereof} and \quotes{if you double the force, you double the velocity gradient}. Modern rheology is more interested in the cases where these simple rules do not hold, and non-linear behaviours dominate the physics. To motivate the rheological results we present later in this work, we first introduce an intuitive and mathematical description of stress and strain. These descriptions do not intend to be a full and rigorous description, for which the reader is referred to one of the many textbooks I cite along the way, but rather to provide a clear and sufficient foundation for interpreting the results we present later in the thesis.

\subsection{Stress and Strain} \label{sec:StressAndStrain}
The concepts of stress and strain constitute the fundamental building blocks of rheology. In considering the flow of a material, strain is defined as a measure of the deformation resulting from the flow, and stress is a measure of the internal forces that result. The simplest definition of strain, therefore, is 
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{L}
    \label{eq:simpleStrain}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x$ is a measure of the deformation and $L$ the systems size \cite{lubliner2008plasticity}. And correspondingly, stress can be defined by the ratio of the internal forces in a material to the cross-sectional area,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma = \frac{F}{A}
    \label{eq:simpleStress}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is a measure of the internal forces and $A$ the cross sectional area \cite{lubliner2008plasticity}. As both stress and strain are dependent on direction in which they are measure compared to the axis/basis on which they are expressed, i.e. the direction of the $\Delta x$ relative to $L$ or the direction of $F$ relative to the normal of $A$, we express them as rank-2 tensors $\bm{\sigma}$ and $\bm{\epsilon}$ respectively. For the strain tensor $\bm{\epsilon}$, we refer to the diagonal elements $i = j$ of $\epsilon_{ij}$ as extensional strains, as $\Delta x$ is measured parallel to $L$, and the off-diagonal $i \neq j$ as shear strains where $\Delta x$ is perpendicular to $L$. Respectively, when $F$ is measured parallel to the normal vector of $A$ ($\sigma_{ij}$ for $i = j$), it is a normal stress; otherwise, if it acts along the plane, it is a shear stress. In \cref{sec:StrainTensor,sec:StressTensor}, we provide a more mathematical construction of both $\bm{\epsilon}$ and $\bm{\sigma}$ respectively, but we refer to Refs. \cite{tannerEngineeringRheology2000, chandrasekharaiahContinuumMechanics2014} for a more detailed treatment.

\subsection{Strain Tensor} \label{sec:StrainTensor}
For simplicity, we will consider the strain tensor $\bm{\epsilon}$ to take the form of Cauchy's linear strain tensor \cite{lubliner2008plasticity}, which will hold throughout this thesis. If we consider a point $P$ which at time $t$ is at $\bm{x}$, which is displaced to a new position $\bm{X}$ at a time $t^\prime$, such that
\begin{equation}
    \bm{X}(t^\prime) = \bm{X}(\bm{x},t).
\end{equation}
Then, considering a nearby point $Q$ which starts at a distance $d\bm{x}$ from point $P$ we can define the relationship between $d\bm{X}$ and $d\bm{x}$ as 
\begin{equation}
    d\bm{X} = \frac{\partial \bm{X}}{\partial \bm{x}}d\bm{x} = \bm{F}d\bm{x}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{F}$ is defined to be the deformation gradient tensor relating $d\bm{X}$ and $d\bm{x}$. To relate the magnitudes of $d\bm{X}$ and $d\bm{x}$ we consider 
\begin{equation}
    dX^2 = d\bm{X} \cdot d\bm{X} = d\bm{X}^T d\bm{X} = d\bm{x}^T\bm{F}^T\bm{F}d\bm{x}.
\end{equation}
The quantity $\bm{C}=\bm{F}^T\bm{F}$ is called the right relative Cauchy-Green Strain tensor\cite{TruesdellNoll1992}.

In order to derive $\bm{\epsilon}$ from $\bm{C}$ we will define the displacement vector 
\begin{equation}
    \bm{u}(\bm{X},t) = \bm{X} - \bm{x}.
\end{equation}
Then the strain tensor $\bm{C}$ can be written in terms of derivatives of the displacement vector
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = F_{ij} - \delta_{ij}, 
\end{equation*}
as 
\begin{equation}
    C_{ij} = \delta_{ij} + \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
    \label{eq:nonlinearstrain}
\end{equation}
where we adopt Einstein notation for convenience and $\delta_{ij}$ is the kronecker delta defined in \cref{symbol:delta}.
In linear elasticity theory, we assume that all displacement gradients are small \cite{TruesdellNoll1992}, such that we can rewrite \cref{eq:nonlinearstrain}
\begin{equation}
        2\epsilon_{ij} \equiv C_{ij} - \delta_{ij} = \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}  + \mathcal{O}\left\{ \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right)^2 \right\}
        \label{eq:linearStrain}
\end{equation}
or in terms of the deformation gradient tensor $\bm{F}$
\begin{equation}
        \epsilon = \frac{1}{2}\left( \bm{F}^T + \bm{F} \right) - \bm{I}.
\end{equation}

The components of $\epsilon_{ij}$ can be separated into two classes: the diagonal components of $\epsilon_{ij}$ describe normal strains, while the off-diagonal elements represent the shear strains. \cref{fig:typesOfStrain} illustrates one diagonal and one off-diagonal element. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \missingfigure[]{Figure showing extensional and simple shear}
    \caption{Figure showing extensional and simple shear}
    \label{fig:typesOfStrain}
\end{figure}

In the context of continuum mechanics, the off-diagonal components of the strain tensor $\epsilon_{ij} (i \neq j)$ represent the \quotes{true} tensorial strains. While these are more suitable for analytical formulations and constitutive modelling, they do not coincide exactly with the strains reported in experiments, which often report the engineering strain as defined in \cref{eq:simpleStrain}. This inconsistency only occurs for shear, where measuring the engineering shear strain in the $xy$ plane as 
\begin{equation*}
    \gamma_{xy} = \frac{\Delta x}{L_y}.
\end{equation*}
would correspond to $\epsilon_{xy} = \epsilon_{yx} = \gamma / 2$.

\subsection{Stress Tensor} \label{sec:StressTensor}
To motivate the definition of the stress tensor $\bm{\sigma}$, we consider a body subjected to a total force $\bm{\mathcal{F}}$. This force may be decomposed into two parts, a body force $\bm{b}$ which acts constantly throughout the volume and a surface force $\bm{F}$ which varies across the material, such that $\bm{\mathcal{F}} = \bm{b} + \bm{F}$ \cite{chandrasekharaiahContinuumMechanics2014}. In the Cauchy formulation of stress, only these surface forces are considered, as the body forces are assumed to cancel across the material \cite{macoskoRheologyPrinciplesMeasurements1994}. Under this assumption, the internal state of the material is defined by the distribution of these surface forces that act on arbitrary cuts through the body. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \missingfigure{Free body diagram. As in Vreeland Fig 1.4}
    \caption{}
    \label{fig:StressFreebody}
\end{figure}

We therefore begin by defining the stress acting on an arbitrary surface element. In \cref{eq:simpleStress}, $\Sigma$ represents the average stress acting on an area $A$; however, we wish to define a local quantity $\bm{\sigma}$ and consider the spatial direction of the stress. To do so, we consider the average force vector $\bm{F}$ acting on $A$ to define,
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{s}_{avg} = \frac{\bm{F}}{A},
\end{equation*}
where $\bm{s}$ denotes the average stress vector acting on $A$. If, now, the area of $A$ is reduced to a point, then the stress at this point on the surface can be considered to be
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{s} = \lim_{A \to 0} \frac{\bm{F}}{A}.
\end{equation*}

To generalise this to any point $P$ in a material can consider making a free-body cut, passing through the point $P$ as shown in \cref{fig:StressFreebody}b. The stress vector $\bm{s}_1$ now denotes the stress acting on the left-hand side of the cut body at $P$. If instead we take an alternative cut, then at the same point $P$ we obtain an alternative stress vector $\bm{s}_2$ (see \cref{fig:StressFreebody}c). In fact, there is an infinite set of cuts we can take through $P$, each with a corresponding stress vector $\bm{s}_i$. This means that each such cut gives only partial information about the {\textit state of stress} at $P$. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \missingfigure{Cauchy Tetrahedron. As in Vreeland Fig 1.5b}
    \caption{}
    \label{fig:CauchyTetrahedron}
\end{figure}

To resolve this, we consider the tetrahedron shown in \cref{fig:CauchyTetrahedron}, made up of three planes parallel to the coordinate axis and a 4\textsuperscript{th} incline plane which connects them \cite{housner1966analysis}. We will denote the planes using their normal vectors $\bm{n}_i$, and the corresponding stress vectors $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_i)}$, where $i$ can take the value $x,\;y,\;z$ or $n$ as denoted in \cref{fig:CauchyTetrahedron}. The stress vectors $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_x)}$, $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_y)}$, $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_z)}$, and $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_n)}$ do not necessarily point parallel to their corresponding planes' normal vector. If we consider the tetrahedron to be infinitesimally small, then the sum of the forces on the tetrahedron must be $\bm{0}$, from Newton's second law. That is 
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_n)} dA_n + \bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_x)} dA_x + \bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_y)} dA_y + \bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_z)} dA_y = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
The area of each of the faces can then be related by projecting the inclined plane onto the perpendicular faces,
\begin{align*}
    dA_x = (\bm{n}_n \cdot \bm{n}_x)dA_n &= l_{xn}dA_n \\
    dA_y = (\bm{n}_n \cdot \bm{n}_y)dA_n &= l_{yn}dA_n \\
    dA_z = (\bm{n}_n \cdot \bm{n}_z)dA_n &= l_{zn}dA_n
\end{align*}
where $\bm{n}_i$ denotes the normal to each plane. By considering the limit as the size of the tetrahedron shrinks, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_n)} = -\left( l_{xn}\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_x)} + l_{yn}\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_y)} + l_{zn}\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_z)} \right).
\end{equation}
The stress vector $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_n)}$ at the point $P$ is therefore realised by the stress on the inclined plane. Thus, we conclude that, in general, in three dimensions, the state of stress at a point is described by three stress vectors. 

While for simplicity we have taken these three vectors to be resolved relative to the coordinate axis, an equivalent description of the stress can be obtained by resolving relative to any three distinct planes which intersect at a point $P$. The rank 2-tensor Cauchy stress tensor $\sigma_{ij}$ can then be constructed by decomposing each stress vector relative to the normal of the defining planes, such that
\begin{equation}
    s^{(\bm{n}_k)}_i = \sigma_{ij}{n_{k,j}}.
\end{equation}
where $\bm{s}^{(\bm{n}_k)}$ denotes the stress vector $\bm{s}$ acting on the plane with normal $\bm{n}_k$. A significant choice of planes defines the principal directions of stress: a set of three mutually orthogonal normals for which the stress tensor $\sigma_{ij}$ is diagonal. For any state of stress, it can be shown that such a principle basis always exists.

If we wish to relate the stress tensor $\epsilon_{ij}$ to the stress tensor $\sigma_{ij}$, then we can consider a linear relation (Hooke's Law) between stress and strain,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkm}\epsilon_{ij}.
\end{equation}
where $C_{ijkm}$ is a four-tensor often called the stiffness or Elasticity tensor. By further applying the constraint that the material must deform isotropically, this reduces to 
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{ij} = \lambda \epsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} + 2G\epsilon_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ and $G$ are moduli (the Lam\'e constants).
This assumption assumes that the stress of a material is solely determined by the deformation of the material, as in a compressible material. If the material is also incompressible, then the stress must be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{ij} + p\delta_{ij} = 2G\epsilon_{ij},
    \label{eq:incompressableStress}
\end{equation}
where $p$ is the pressure.

Finally, when analysing the response of the materials in this thesis, we discuss the bulk stress $\bm{\Sigma}$ and strain $\bm{\epsilon}$. Although this distinction can be a source of confusion, we keep the same notation for the local and bulk strain tensors $\bm{\epsilon}$, under the assumption that the material deforms homogenously. In the cases where this assumption breaks down, the implications of this will be discussed. By contrast, we distinguish between local and bulk measures of stress. The bulk stress tensor is denoted by $\Sigma$ to emphasise that it represents a spatially averaged quantity, which may differ from the local stress tensor $\sigma$ due to non-linear effects such as plasticity, which we discuss next.

\subsection{Plasticity} \label{sec:Plasticity}

The description of stress and strain introduced above implicitly assumes an elastic response, such that the stress is a single-valued function of only the instantaneous strain. However, for many soft materials, this assumption breaks down once the deformation exceeds a characteristic elastic limit. Beyond this point, irreversible microscopic rearrangements occur, which manifest macroscopically as plastic deformation or fracture, introducing dissipation and history dependence in the system's response.

In contrast to elastic deformation, where, upon unloading, all strain is recovered and the material returns to its initial configuration, plastic deformation causes a permanent, irreversible change in the material's configuration. Consequently, upon unloading, the material does not return to its original state, and the material retains a memory of its deformation history in its new configuration. Microscopically theses deformation may correspond to particle rearrangements \cite{falk_dynamics_1998,maloney_amorphous_2006,tanguy_plastic_2006,gartner_nonlinear_2016}, dislocation \cite{shiba_plastic_2010, ispanovity_avalanches_2014,ben-zion_earthquake_1993}, or bond rupture \cite{tauber_stretchy_2022,ducrot_toughening_2014,Creton50th2017,vernereyStatisticalDamageMechanics2018,gong_why_2010} which all reduce the total stored elastic energy.  Rheologically, the sum of these microscopic deformations manifests as stress relaxation, yielding, and flow under sustained loading. 

\subsubsection{Shear Banding} \label{sec:ShearBanding}
An emergent phenomenon of many of these soft elastoplastic materials is the formation of \quotes{shear banding}, whereby the materials' flow profile separates into multiple co-existing macroscopic regions with different strain rates \cite{divoux_stress_2011,divouxTransientShearBanding2010,divoux_yielding_2012,grenard_timescales_2014,gibaud_heterogeneous_2010,kurokawa_avalanche-like_2015}. Such banding has been extensively observed experimentally in a range of amorphous solids \cite{berretNonlinearRheologyTelechelic2001,berretTransientRheologyWormlike1997,besselingShearBandingFlowConcentration2010,diatEffectShearLyotropic1993,divouxShearBandingComplex2016,divouxTransientShearBanding2010,goyonSpatialCooperativitySoft2008,rogersAgingYieldingShear2008,tapadiaBandingEntangledPolymer2006}. In many cases the bands heal over time, leaving a homogeneously fluidised state; however, in some materials shear banding is implicated in catastrophic failure \cite{falk_deformation_2011,bonn_yield_2017,larson_instabilities_1992,OlmstedShearBanding2008,bonnDelayedFractureInhomogeneous1998,tabuteauMicroscopicMechanismsBrittleness2009}. Harder materials, such as polymeric and metallic glasses, show similar phenomenology, yielding via strain localisation and shear banding \cite{greer_shear_2013,hufnagel_deformation_2016,boyce_large_1988,anand_large-deformation_2012,chen_mechanical_2008}. Here the formation of shear bands is typically associated with brittle yielding catastrophic failure, as the material rapidly fractures along the band. 

While the resultant steady state flow of these materials has been widely studied, with a unifying picture of locally correlated plastic events form avalanches of activity across the system \cite{liuDrivingRateDependence2016,puosi_time-dependent_2014,linDensityShearTransformations2014,tanguyPlasticResponse2D2006,lin_scaling_2014,maloney_amorphous_2006}, the onset of these bands remains debated. This initial onset has been widely studied with approaches such as a replica field theory \cite{jaiswalMechanicalYieldAmorphous2016}, as a critical point in an elastoplastic model \cite{linCriticalityApproachFailure2015,liuCreepDynamicsAthermal2018}, as a percolation transition \cite{shrivastavYieldingGlassShear2016}, within artificially seeded particle simulations \cite{ozawaRareEventsDisorder2022}, and experimentally \cite{aimeMicroscopicDynamicsFailure2018,aimeStresscontrolledShearCell2016,knowltonMicroscopicViewYielding2014}.


As most of the work in this thesis concerns the fracture of networks, we will generally use \quotes{shear banding} to denote localised bands of high deformation, as is done in metallic glasses. In places where it is important to distinguish between these localised bands and the macroscopic regions of different strain rate, and it is not clear from context, we will do so.


\section{Rheological protocol} \label{sec:Protocols}
There are many methods and protocols by which flow can be induced in a material. In this work, we adopt a set of strain-controlled deformation protocols. That is, at any point during our simulation, a prescribed bulk deformation is imposed, and the resulting stress response is measured. Alternatively, in a stress-controlled protocol, a fixed (though potentially time-dependent) stress is imposed, and the material is allowed to deform accordingly \cite{macoskoRheologyPrinciplesMeasurements1994}. 

Within the system's elastic regime, these two approaches are equivalent, as stress and strain are uniquely related through the elastic moduli. Beyond the onset of plasticity, however, their responses differ. In a stress-controlled protocol, yielding can result in unbounded deformation, and as such, is therefore useful for exploring behaviours such as creep. Consequently, strain-controlled protocols, by definition, remain bounded in strain; upon yielding, the magnitude of stress strictly decreases toward 0. Consequently, strain-controlled regimes are often used to study yielding and stress redistribution, which is the focus of this thesis.

We will consider two forms of deformation in this thesis: Simple shear is used in \cref{sec:Fatigue,sec:Ultradelayed} and Pure shear in \cref{sec:DoubleNetworks}. Further, each chapter will use a different strain-controlled protocol to apply its relevant deformation. 

\subsubsection{Types of deformation} \label{sec:TypesDeformation}
The two forms of deformation we will consider in this thesis are: simple shear and pure shear. Simple shear is a volume-preserving deformation where parallel planes slide past one another without changing their separation. By contrast, pure shear is characterised by a simultaneous extension along one axis and compression along the orthogonal axis, such that the total area (in two dimensions) is conserved. These two deformation modes are illustrated in \cref{fig:simpleAndPureShear}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \missingfigure[]{Figure illustrating Pure and simple shear}
    \caption{Figure illustrating Pure and simple shear. {\bf a)} Illustration of simple shear applied along the $xy$ plane. {\bf b)} Illustration of pure shear with elongational axis along the $y$-axis.}
    \label{fig:simpleAndPureShear}

Both deformation modes preserve the overall area of the material; they do so by inducing contrasting forms of deformation. Simple shear is characterised by non-zero off-diagonal components of the stress tensor $\bm{\sigma}$, which induce a rotation of principal stress axes of the material. In fact, simple shear can be decomposed into a combination of pure shear and a rigid-body rotation. Pure shear, therefore, contains no such rotational component, and the principal directions of stress remain fixed to the axes of deformation.

\section{Hydrodynamics} \label{sec:Hydrodynamics}

By considering the conservation of mass and momentum, the Navier-Stokes equations for a generalised incompressible flow can be expressed as,

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:NavierStokes}
\begin{align}
    \rho \left(\partial_t+(\bm{u} \cdot \nabla)\right)\bm{u} &= \nabla \cdot \bm{\Sigma} \label{eq:incompressibleNavier1} \\
    \nabla\cdot\bm{u} &= 0 \label{eq:incompressibleNavier2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

where $\rho$ is the fluid's density, $\bm{u}$ the fluid's flow field, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a tensor which characterises the total stress applied to the fluid. The total stress in the fluid can be decomposed into two components $\bm{\Sigma} = \bm{\sigma} - p\bm{I}$, where $p$ is the hydrostatic pressure and $\bm{\sigma}$ the deviatoric stress tensor. 
The physics we wish to consider in this thesis is the slow flow of pasty materials where the internal viscous forces dominate over the inertial forces. To do this, we take the limit of \cref{eq:incompressibleNavier1} as $Re=\rho |\bm{v}| L / \mu -> 0$ where $L$ is the typical length scale for the fluid. In this limit, the right-hand side of the \cref{eq:incompressibleNavier1} vanishes, and the Navier-Stokes equations reduce to,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:StokesLimit}
\begin{align}
    \nabla \cdot (\bm{\sigma} - p\bm{I}) &= 0 \\
    \nabla \cdot \bm{u} &= 0.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
This can also be derived directly by considering the equilibrium state \cref{eq:incompressableStress}, that is, the net force in the system $\nabla \cdot \mu\bm{\epsilon} = \nabla \cdot \bm{\Sigma} = 0$.

Following the arguments of Ref. \cite{picard_elastic_2004} we will assume that the strain $\bm{\epsilon}$ is as linear superposition of a plastic flow $\bm{\epsilon}^{pl}$ and elastic deformation $\bm{\epsilon^{el}}$ and that the fluid is homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. The strain $\bm{\epsilon}$ of the fluid can be therefore be expressed as 
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\epsilon}= \bm{\epsilon^{el}} + \bm{\epsilon}^{pl}, \quad \text{and, }\bm{\epsilon^{el}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla \bm{u} + \left( \nabla \bm{u} \right)^T \right)
    \label{eq:decompositionAndDeformation}
\end{equation} 
where $\bm{u}(\bm{r})$ is the total displacement vector at a position $\bm{r}$.
In addition, for simplicity, we will consider the case where the fluid is incompressible $\nabla \cdot \bm{u} = 0$ such that the stress $\bm{\sigma} = \mu$. The deviatoric stress tensor $\bm{\sigma}$ can therefore be expressed as 
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\sigma} = \mu \bm{\epsilon^{el}} = \mu\bm{\epsilon} - \mu\bm{\epsilon}^{pl}.
    \label{eq:elasticStress}
\end{equation}

Considering a single localised plastic event induced by elastic loading, the strain field $\bm{\epsilon}$ can be decomposed into a superposition of $\bm{\epsilon}^{el0}$, $\bm{\epsilon}^{el1}$ and $\bm{\epsilon}^{pl1}$ where the superscript $0$ denotes the contribution from the elastic loading and $1$ the contribution from the localised plastic event. The materials deformation field can also be decomposed in the same way into a pair of deformation fields $\bm{u}^0$ and $\bm{u}^1$, which are the material's elastic and plastic responses, respectively, again by superposition $\bm{u} = \bm{u}^0 + \bm{u}^1$. As the elastic contribution must obey \cref{eq:StokesLimit} and the incompressibility condition $\nabla \cdot \bm{u}^0 = 0$, the plastic deformation $\bm{u}^1$ resulting from the localised plastic event must satisfy, 
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:plasticStokes}
    \begin{align}
        \nabla \cdot \left( \mu \bm{\epsilon}^{el1} - p^1 \bm{I} \right) &= \mu \nabla \cdot \bm{\epsilon}^{pl1}, \label{eq:plasticStokes1}\\
        \nabla \cdot \bm{u}^1 &= 0.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Under the assumption that the deformation is isotropic and $\nabla \cdot \bm{\epsilon} = \bm{0}$ for any deformation at rest, we can express the right hand side of \cref{eq:plasticStokes1} as $\nabla \cdot \left( \mu \bm{\epsilon}^{el1} - p^1 \bm{I} \right) = -\nabla p^1 + \mu \nabla^2 \bm{u}^1$

\subsection{Oseen/Stokeslet Tensor} \label{sec:Oseen}
The solution to \cref{eq:plasticStokes} is well known and has been studied in a wide range of systems. The most common way to approach the problem is to consider the response to a point force $F$ applied at $\bm{r^\prime}$ in a periodic system,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:Stokes}
\begin{align}
- \nabla p(\bm{r}) + \mu \nabla^2 \bm{u}(\bm{r}) &= -\bm{F} \delta(\bm{r^\prime}), \label{eq:Stokes1} \\
\nabla \cdot \bm{u} &= 0. \label{eq:Stokes2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the solution to the deformation field can be expressed as a convolution of the point force with the Green's function $\mathbb{O}_{ij}(\bm{r},\bm{r^\prime})$ of the system, 
\begin{equation}
    \bm{u}(\bm{r}) = \int \bm{\mathbb{O}}(\bm{r},\bm{r^\prime}) \bm{F} \delta(\bm{r^\prime}).
\end{equation}
The tensor $\bm{\mathbb{O}}$ was first derived by Oseen in 1927 \cite{oseen_neuere_1927}, refined by Burgers in 1939 \cite{Burgers1939}, and is commonly referred to as the Oseen-Burgers tensor or the Stokeslet tensor \cite{Hancock1953}, depending on the field of the author. It is important to note here that the form of \cref{eq:plasticStokes} echoes the work of Eshelby \cite{eshelby_determination_1997}, who considered the effect of a \quotes{plastic} deformation of an elliptical inclusion with constant value inside and zero outside, on an infinite elastic sheet. In this work, Eshelby showed that the displacement caused by this \quotes{plastic} event can be considered as if generated by the boundary of the inclusion, which is equivalent to \cref{eq:plasticStokes}.

\subsubsection{Derivation}
Inspired by Ref. \cite{kim_microhydrodynamics_1991}, we enforce that the deformation field $\bm{u}(\bm{r})$ of \cref{eq:Stokes} vanishes at infinity, i.e. $\lim_{r \to 0}|\bm{u}(\bm{r})| = 0$. Under this assumption and using that the Stokes equations are linear in $p(\bm{r})$ and $\bm{u}(\bm{r})$, we can write the pressure field as the scalar product of the point force $\bm{F}$ and a vector field $\bm{P}(\bm{r})$ and the deformation field $\bm{u}(\bm{r})$ as the tensor $\mathbb{O}_{ij}$ acting on $F$:
\begin{equation}
    p(\bm{r}) = P_j F_j, \qquad
    u_i(\bm{r}) = \mathbb{O}_{ij} F_j.
\end{equation}
Applying the fourier transform to \cref{eq:Stokes}, we obtain 
\begin{equation}
- i k_i \hat{P}_j F_j 
- k^2 \hat{\mathbb{O}}_{ij} F_j 
= -F_i = -F_j \delta_{ij}^{K}.
\label{eq:StokesFourier}
\end{equation}
By cancelling $F_j$ and multiplying by $k_i$ we can apply the incompressibility condition $ik_i\hat{u}_i=ik_i\hat{\mathbb{O}}_{ij} F_j=0$ to obtain
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{P}_j = -\frac{ik_j}{k^2}.
\end{equation*}
Substituting $\hat{P}_j$ back into \cref{eq:StokesFourier} we find a closed expression for $\bm{\hat{\mathbb{O}}}$ is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbb{O}}_{ij} = \frac{1}{\mu k^2}\left( \delta_{ij} - \frac{k_ik_j}{k^2} \right).
    \label{eq:Oseen}
\end{equation}
This gives an expression for the deformation as 
\begin{equation}
\hat{u}_i = \frac{1}{\mu k^2}\left( \delta_{ij} - \frac{k_ik_j}{k^2} \right)F_j,
\label{eq:fourierDeformation}
\end{equation}
by taking the partial derivative ($\partial_l$) of $\hat{u}_i $ which is equivalent to multiplying by $ik_i$ in fourier Space we find that deformation gradients ($\partial_l u_i$) can be expressed as 
\begin{equation}
    ik_l\hat{u}_i = \frac{ik_l}{\mu k^2}\left( \delta_{ij} - \frac{k_ik_j}{k^2} \right)F_j
    \label{eq:fourierDeformationGradient}
\end{equation}
Both \cref{eq:fourierDeformation,eq:fourierDeformationGradient} naturally describe the deformation and gradient fields for a periodic system up to an arbitrary constant $\bm{u}_0$, due to the singularity at $\bm{k}=\bm{0}$. 

\subsection{Eshelby Stress Propagator} \label{sec:EshelbyPropogator}

The associated stress and strain fields are then obtained by substituting this result for $\partial_l u_i$ into \cref{eq:decompositionAndDeformation,eq:elasticStress} in fourier space, giving the resultant Shear stress correction in
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}_{x y}^1 = \mu^2\left(q_y^2 \hat{\mathbb{O}}_{x x}+q_x^2 \hat{\mathbb{O}}_{y y}+2 q_x q_y \hat{\mathbb{O}}_{x y}-1 / \mu\right) \hat{\epsilon}_{x y}^{\mathrm{pl1}} = \mu \hat{\mathbb{ G }}_{ xy } \hat{\epsilon}_{x y}^{\mathrm{pl1}}.
\end{equation}
By collecting terms, we can compute the shear stress propagator $\mathbb{G}_{xy}$, which expresses the consequences of a single plastic event in an infinite medium by,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{x y}^1(\bm{r}) = \mu \int d\bm{r}^\prime \mathbb{G}_{xy} (\bm{r} - \bm{r}^\prime) \epsilon_{x y}^{\mathrm{pl1}}(\bm{r}^\prime)
\end{equation}
where, 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbb{ G }}_{ xy } = -4 \frac{ k_x^2 k_y^2 }{ k^4 } \quad \text{ and } \quad \hat{\mathbb{ G }}_{ xy } = -4 \frac{ k_x^2 k_y^2 }{ k^4 } - \frac{ k_z^2 }{ k^2 } \quad \text{ for } \bm{k} \neq \bm{0}
    \label{eq:StressPropogator}
\end{equation}
in two ($D=2$) and three ($D=3$) dimensions respectively.
Expressing both of these equations along the $xy$ plane in $\mathbb{R}^2$ we find that 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{G}_{xy} \sim \frac{1}{\pi r^{D}}\cos{4\theta}
    \label{eq:eshelbyReal}
\end{equation}
as described by Eshelby in Ref. \cite{eshelby_determination_1997} for an elliptical inclusion. The Eshelby Stress propagator defined in \cref{eq:StressPropogator}, is only defined up to a constant due to the singularity at $\bm{k}=\bm{0}$. In practice, this constant is defined by the boundary conditions of the system, where, in a strain-controlled regime, the elastic strain in the system is conserved. As such, the singularity at $\bm{k}=\bm{0}$ is resolved by setting $\hat{\mathbb{G}}_{xy}(\bm{0}) = -1$ such that the total stress lost in the system due to plastic events is $\mu\int d\bm{r}^\prime \epsilon_{x}^{\mathrm{pl1}}(\bm{r}^\prime)$. In a stress control regime, it is common to instead take $\hat{\mathbb{G}}_{xy}(\bm{0}) = 0$ where no total stress is lost due to the event and instead the lost plastic stress is converted to elastic stress by the system elasticity straining\cite{nicolas_deformation_2018, martens_spontaneous_2012, jocteur_protocol_2025, lin_scaling_2014}.

\section{Soft Networks} \label{sec:Networks}

\subsection{Types of Network} \label{sec:TypesOfNetworks}
With the term \quotes{soft networks}, we describe a whole class of materials. These materials all share the common feature of being composed of a network of interconnected filaments; however, their mechanical properties vary widely. While we endeavoured to make our models as general as possible, it is important to understand the differences between these materials and how these differences may affect the applicability of our results. Below, we briefly describe some of the most common types of soft networks.

\paragraph{Elastomers}
Composed of long flexible polymer chains cross-linked into a network, elastomers are perhaps the most well-known class of soft networks, found in thermosetting rubbers and plastic. Existing above the glass transition temperature $T_g$ of polymer networks, their mechanical behaviour is largely governed by entropic elasticity: deformation reduces the configurational entropy of polymer chains, generating a restoring force \cite{treloarElasticityRelatedProperties1973}. As a result, elastomers typically exhibit large reversible deformations until bond or cross-linker failure occurs, with pronounced strain stiffening prior to failure \cite{kimMeasurementNonlinearMechanical2011}.

\paragraph{Hydrogels}
As the name implies Hydrogels are a type of polymer gel embedded in water, which can form up to $90\%$ of the material volume. This tends to leave them softer and weaker than elastomer due to there lower polymer density. This means that there is a significant reduction in interaction between the polymers below the network level, meaning there properties are highly dependent on the network structure. However, the presence of solvent also introduces new physical phenomena such as the swelling of polymer chains and a coupling between network deformation and fluid flow referred to as poroelasticity \cite{biotTheoryFiniteDeformations1972}. Due to their high solvent content, hydrogels are often used in biomedical applications \cite{yasudaNovelDoubleNetworkHydrogel2009,yinSelfHealingHydrogelsSynthesis2023,hoHydrogelsPropertiesApplications2022}.

\paragraph{Fibre networks}
Mainly biological networks these networks are composed of semi-flexible fibres such as actin and collagen. As a result these networks tend to have a length scale much larger than elastomers and hydrogels, with mesh sizes on the order of $1 \mu m$ to $10 \mu m$ \cite{mackintosh_elasticity_1995}. Much like hydrogels, these networks are highly porous, with a large fraction of their volume being occupied by solvent, however, due to their larger mesh size they do not exhibit significant poroelastic effects \cite{burlaMechanicalResilienceActive2019}. 

Due to the nature of these networks, the type of the crosslinks in fibre networks can vary widely from linkages via flexible proteins to intricate branching and interweaving of fibre bundles.

\subsection{Semi-Flexible Filaments} \label{sec:SemiFlexible}
Polymer fibres can be separated into three categories by the ratio of their persistence length $l_p$ to their relevant contour length $l_c$ (in the case of networks, the relevant length is the average length between crosslinks). The persistence length is a measure of the rigidity of the polymer and defines the length at which the polymer's orientation remains correlated. Therefore, the persistence length of a polymer can be intuitively thought of as the length at which thermal energy, $k_{B}T$, is large enough to have an effect on the direction of the polymer (formally defined as the length at which the correlation between the two tangent equals $1/e$) and can be defined by $l_p = \kappa / (k_{B}T)$.

For short or stiff polymers where $l_p \ll l_c$, the polymer acts like a rigid rod with its mechanical response governed by the polymer's stretching of molecular bonds. Polymers where $l_p \gg l_c$ take a naturally folded state as the thermal energy is sufficient to bend the filament significantly, these polymers have mechanical responses driven by entropy where the initial response is governed by the reduction of accessible conformations as the polymer unfolds, followed by a regime governed by the stretching of molecular bonds as in the stiff case. Semi-flexible polymers, therefore, constitute an intermediate state between the two, where their persistence length $l_p$ is large enough relative to their contour length $l_c$ to avoid collapsing into a random coil but large enough that thermally induced bending remains relevant. 

The standard model for polymers is the extensible worm-like chain, where each polymer is treated as a homogeneous cylindrical elastic rod with radius $r$ and Young's modulus $E$ \cite{doi_theory_1988}. As such, the stretching rigidity of the rod can be expressed in terms of the product of its Young's modulus and cross-sectional area $\mu = E \pi r^2$ and its bending rigidity in terms of its Young's modulus and moment of inertia $\kappa = E I = E \pi r^4 / 4$. The Hamiltonian of a single filament can therefore be written as the sum of two integrals 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H} = \frac{\mu}{2} \int ds \left( \frac{dl}{ds} \right)^2 + \frac{\kappa}{2} \int ds \left| \frac{\partial \vec{t}}{\partial s} \right|^2
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the arc length coordinate, $dl/ds$ is the relative strain of the polymer at point $s$ and $\vec{t}$ is the tangent vector to the polymer. We neglect any energy contributions from the polymers twisting along their axis. Numerically, we discretise each filament into a series of segments \cite{mackintosh_elasticity_1995} for which the Hamiltonian of each filament can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H} = \frac{\mu}{2} \sum_{ij} \frac{( l_{ij} - l_{ij,0} )^2}{l_{ij,0}} + \frac{\kappa}{2} \sum_{ijk} \frac{( \theta_{ijk} - \theta_{ijk,0} )^2}{l_{ijk,0}}
    \label{eq:NetworkHamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where the first sum is taken over all pairs $ij$ and triplets $ijk$ along the polymer length. We define $l_{ij}$ and $l_{ij,0}$ as the instantaneous and natural length between nodes $i$ and $j$ respectively, $\theta_{ijk}$ and $\theta_{ijk,0}$ to be the instances and natural angle between nodes  $i$, $j$ and $k$, respectively, and $l_{ijk,0} = (l_{ij,0} + l_{jk,0})/2$. In all cases, we consider a central force model only where bending rigidity is neglected; this can be thought of as the polymers having a small cross-sectional area relative to their length, such that their moment of inertia is negligible compared to their cross-sectional area.

\subsection{Dynamics} \label{sec:NetworkDynamics}
To resolve the dynamics of the networks in this work, we will consider the system to remain in force balance with a background solvent such that the force on each node of the network as a result of the bond $\bm{F}_{n,i}=\partial \mathcal{H} / \partial \bm{r}_i$ is opposed by a drag force from the solvent $\bm{F}_{d,i}$ such that $\bm{F}_{d,i} + \bm{F}_{n,i}=\bm{0}$. For the drag term, we assume no long-range hydrodynamic interactions due to hydrodynamic screening, which occurs within the densely packed system \cite{shankar_theory_2002, visscher_dynamic_1994}. Instead, we consider a background Newtonian fluid with velocity field $\bm{v}_f(\bm{r}_i)$ which exerts a force on the node 
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{F}_{d, i} = -\zeta(\bm{\dot{r}_i} - \bm{v}_f(\bm{r}_i))
\end{equation*}
where $\zeta$ is a drag coefficient, which we will choose $\zeta=1$ to define our units of time. It should be noted that this drag term violates Galilean invariance. However, this form is commonly used in literature \cite{gittes_dynamic_1998, broedersz_modeling_2014, ikeda_unified_2012, puosi_time-dependent_2014,durian_foam_1995, salerno_avalanches_2012, liu_driving_2016} and still provides similar results when compared to the more accurate form used in dissipative particle dynamics \cite{groot_dissipative_1997}. Deformation in the system can then be considered to be applied through the widely-used \quotes{affine solvent model} \cite{durian_foam_1995,lerner_simulations_2013} which assumes the background fluid affinely deforms in response to the macroscopically applied strain.

\subsubsection{Integration Method} \label{sec:EulerHeun}
To resolve the dynamics described above, we will consider a splitting scheme for each particle's equation of motion
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{\dot{r}_i} = \frac{1}{\zeta}\bm{F}_{n,i} + \bm{v}_f(\bm{r}_i).
\end{equation*}
We decompose the particles' displacement over a time step $\delta t$ into two sequential contributions: macroscopic advection and microscopic interaction. 

First, each particle's position and the system's unit cell are affinely deformed under $\bm{v}_f$. This intermediate advected position, $\bm{r}^\prime_i$, is calculated as:
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{r}^\prime_i = \bm{r}_i(t) + \delta t \bm{v}_f(\bm{r}_i(t)).
\end{equation*}
Subsequently, we employ an adaptive Euler-Heun method (also known as the Runge-Kutta 12 method) inspired by Ref.\cite{sammullerAdaptiveBrownianDynamics2021}, which used its embedded Runge-Kutta pair to estimate the local discretisation error at each time step to dynamically adjust its step size $h$. Using the advected position $\bm{r}^\prime_i$ as the initial condition, we first compute a predictor estimate $\tilde{\bm{r}}_i$:
\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{\bm{r}}_i = \bm{r}^\prime_i + \frac{\delta t}{\zeta} \bm{F}_{n,i}(\bm{r}^\prime).
\end{equation*}
The forces are re-evaluated at this predicted position to compute the final corrected position for the time step $t + \delta t$:
\begin{equation*}
    \bm{r}_i(t + \delta t) = \bm{r}^\prime_i + \frac{h}{2\zeta} \left[ \bm{F}_{n,i}( \bm{r}^\prime_i ) + \bm{F}_{n,i}(\tilde{\bm{r}_i}) \right].
\end{equation*}
The particle-wise error can be defined as 
\begin{equation*}
    E_i = \left\| \Delta\tilde{\bm{r}}_i - \Delta\bm{r}_i \right\| = \frac{\delta t}{2\zeta}\left\| \bm{F}_{n,i}(\tilde{\bm{r}_i}) - \bm{F}_{n,i}( \bm{r}^\prime_i ) \right\|_2
\end{equation*}
with $\Delta\tilde{\bm{r}}_i = \tilde{\bm{r}}_i - \bm{r}^\prime_i$, $\Delta\bm{r}_i = \bm{r}_i(t+h) - \bm{r}_i(t)$ and $\left\| \cdot\right\|_2$ denoting the two-norm. We introduce an error tolerance $\tau$, which we will use to constrain the maximum allowed absolute error. The total error estimate is then
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{E} = \left\| \frac{E_i}{\tau} \right\|_\infty
\end{equation*}
with $\left\| \cdot\right\|_\infty$ denoting the $\infty$-norm taken such that we constrain the maximum single-particle absolute error. Following the arguments in Ref.\cite{sammullerAdaptiveBrownianDynamics2021} we construct a scaling factor for $\delta t$
\begin{equation*}
    q = \left( \frac{1}{2\mathcal{E}} \right)^2
\end{equation*}
which we restrict to be in the range $[0.001, 1.2]$. With the choice $q$, one can distinguish two possible scenarios in each step:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $q \geq 1$: Accept $\bm{r}_i (t + \delta t)$ as correct.
    \item $q < 1$: Reject $\bm{r}_i(t + \delta t)$ and reset the particles position to $\bm{r}_i(t)$ to retry with a smaller step.
\end{itemize}
In both cases, the time step is adapted afterwards via $\delta t \gets q \delta t$. We also impose a maximum time step $\delta t_{max}$ such that we are still able to resolve the temporal resolution of the fractures.

One of the most fundamental properties of these interconnected fibre networks is the rigidity of the network, both on a local and global scale. Consider first frameworks where each connection in our network has a fixed length and is allowed to freely hinge around the connecting nodes. (The limit as $\mu \to \infty$ and $\kappa \to 0$.) Defining $\bm{r_i(t)}$ to be the position of the $i$th node of the system then we can define our network through the set of equations $|\bm{r}_i(t) - \bm{r}_j(t)|^2 = l_{ij,0}^2$ for all connected pairs $ij$. By differentiating with respect to $t$, a set of constraints can be generated,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:FrameworkRigidity}
    (\bm{\dot{r}}_i(t) - \bm{\dot{r}}_j(t))\cdot(\bm{r}_i(t) - \bm{r}_j(t))=0 \text{ for all pairs } ij
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\dot{r}_i(t)}$ is the velocity of the node $i$ at time $t$. If it is possible to find a non-trivial set of velocities which satisfies \cref{eq:FrameworkRigidity}, then the framework can move infinitesimally as at least one node can be moved freely. This defines the most strict definition of rigidity where for a given network we define it to be rigid only if and only if the solution to \cref{eq:FrameworkRigidity} is the trivial solution of $\dot{r}_i(t) = 0$ for all $i$. This idea is more simply understood through the work of Maxwell \cite{maxwell_calculation_2011} who initially pointed out that this definition of rigidity is directly related to the number of constraints in the system, where for a $d$-dimensional system of $N$ nodes the condition for the number of constraints $N_c$  for the system to be rigid is $N_c \geq dN - d(d+1)/2$. Assuming that the network is homogeneous so we can define the average connectivity of the network as $z = 2N_c/N$ and letting $N\to\infty$, then the simple criterion for rigidity is $z\geq 2d$, where $z_c = 2d$ is often referred to as the isostatic point. This statement is only valid when there is one rigid cluster and all bonds are independent. However, this is often not the case for over-constrained systems. In these cases, the network contains areas of self-stress, which are configurations of internal forces that are in equilibrium, where the actual number of degrees of freedom and constraints in these systems is different from just counting the number of nodes and connections \cite{calladine_buckminster_1978}.

This definition is often over-prescriptive and does not work in non-homogeneous networks where the average connectivity $z$ does not provide enough information to define if the network is rigid. For example, if we consider attaching a single freely hanging node to an otherwise rigid network, then we disrupt the overall definition of rigidity as this new node is now free to rotate without affecting the overall global rigidity of the network and its ability to retain stress. For such a problem, we need to consider where the constraints are located within the system to determine the global rigidity of the system. We also need to consider the case of sparsely connected networks below the isostatic point, where we find small clusters of rigid nodes connected by floppy under-constrained regions. In extremely non-homogeneous cases, these networks can have connectivity greater than $z_c=2d$, rearrange such that the global deformation does not require the extension of any bonds. As such, a network only requires a single system-spanning rigid cluster to hold regions of self-stress, this is why the identification of the rigidity transition is referred to as rigidity percolation \cite{zhang_correlated_2019}. This should not be confused with geometrical percolation, which identifies when the top and bottom of a network are connected to each other \cite{kendall_percolation_2009,broadbent_percolation_1957}. In two dimensions this problem has been well studied \cite{ellenbroek_rigidity_2015} through algorithms such as the pebble game \cite{jacobs_algorithm_1997, jacobs_generic_1995}. 

All these considerations only describe a system's \quotes{structural} or \quotes{first-order rigidity}, where the structure of the network inhibits the motion of the system on both a global and local level. It is well known that under-constrained spring networks can undergo a rigidity transition at coordination number less than $z_c=2d$ \cite{feng_percolation_1984, sharma_strain-controlled_2016, wyart_elasticity_2008, feng_nonlinear_2016, vermeulen_geometry_2017, rens_micromechanical_2018}. The simplest example of such a transition is a simple string with fixed ends. When the two fixed ends are closer together than the length of the string, the string provides no resistance to its deformation and can be classed as floppy. However, once the fixed points are moved far enough apart that the string is now in tension, it can resist deflection and thus appears to be rigid \cite{biot_mechanics_1965}. We refer to this as \quotes{second-order} rigidity \cite{connelly_second-order_1996}, where non-structural changes can cause the system to act as if it is in a rigid configuration. Second-order rigidity is mathematically expressed by the differentiation of \cref{eq:FrameworkRigidity} to find,
\begin{equation*}
    (\bm{r}_i(t) - \bm{r}_j(t))\cdot(\bm{\ddot{r}}_i(t) - \bm{\ddot{r}}_j(t)) + (\bm{\dot{r}}_i(t) - \bm{\dot{r}}_j(t))\cdot(\bm{\dot{r}}_i(t) - \bm{\dot{r}}_j(t))=0
\end{equation*}
where $\bm{\dot{r}_i(t)}$ is the acceleration of the node $i$ at time $t$. This allows for rigidity to occur when there are non-trivial solutions to \cref{eq:FrameworkRigidity} due to force balances in the material. This provides a weaker form of rigidity but still allows for the network to hold regions of self-stress. These configurations are much harder to predict \cite{holmes-cerfon_almost-rigidity_2021} as they arise from non-linear effects which have been highlighted in the context of the frameworks and tensegrities \cite{connelly_frameworks_2022, connelly_second-order_1996, calladine_first-order_1991}. In fact, determining whether a planar network of springs is rigid is NP-hard \cite{abbott_generalizations_2008}, and as such, there is no simple theory that can determine if the system is truly rigid.

For a system of semi-flexible fibres where each fibre can naturally extend, both first and second-order rigidity provide a reasonable proxy for the rigidity of the system \cite{van_hecke_jamming_2010}. However, they are unable to fully capture the complexity of the network. A more recent and intuitive idea of \quotes{energetic rigidity} \cite{damavandi_energetic_2022} has provided a more relevant idea to the rigidity of fibre networks. Energetic rigidity defines a rigid state to be one where any small deformation increases the elastic energy of the structure, or, more simply, where the network resides in a local energy minimum This idea has provided valuable insights into the rigidity of spring networks, vertex models, and jammed packings of soft particles \cite{damavandi_energetic_2022-1}, especially in the context of prestressed systems. However, this still does not provide a comprehensive framework to predict the rigidity of the system.

\subsection{Disordered Network Models} \label{sec:NetworkStructure}
Through the literature, a wide range of network geometries has been considered. The simplest are Affine isotropic models, which consider the response of a single filament to an applied deformation, averaging over all possible initial filament orientations, such as those studied in \cite{mackintosh_elasticity_1995, storm_nonlinear_2005}. While these models can be used to provide a baseline understanding for networks in the high $z$ or $\kappa$ limits, they fail to capture the important physics which arises from the non-affine rearrangements and orientational disorder found in real-world networks. To consider these effects, we must prescribe a network topology which specifies how the bonds connect the nodes of the network.

\subsubsection{Lattice-based networks} \label{sec:LatticeBasedNetworks}
Lattice-based networks are the simplest type of network structures that are commonly used in studies of mechanical properties \cite{jacobs_generic_1995, feng_percolation_1984, shivers_scaling_2019, sharma_strain-controlled_2016}. Frequently considered examples of lattice-based networks include two-dimensional square \cite{mao_soft_2010}, triangular \cite{broedersz_criticality_2011, feng_percolation_1984}, and honeycomb \cite{rens_nonlinear_2016} lattices. Often used for their simplicity to construct when compared to more disordered network types, and their relative numerical stability due to the uniform lattice spacing $l_0$, they provide a convenient and well-controlled framework for analysing the mechanical response of fibre networks. 

Such networks are constructed by placing nodes on a regularly defined grid defined by a set of lattice vectors 
and then subsequently connecting these nodes according to a prescribed set of rules. For instance, in a 2-dimensional square lattice, the node positions can be defined by the lattice vectors $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, and by connecting nearest-neighbour nodes vertically and horizontally, respecting boundary conditions as needed. Other simple lattices, such as the triangular lattice, can be generated analogously, with more complicated lattices may be derived from these fundamental cases. As an example, a hexagonal lattice can be derived from a triangular lattice by imposing an added constraint that each node has a connectivity $z = 3$. Owing to their natural structure, such lattices exhibit highly predictable behaviour, characterised by discrete values in their the connectivity and bond lengths.

This predictability is not desired in disordered networks, where we wish to look at amorphous materials with no long-range structure. Disorder is therefore introduced into these networks through two complementary methods. Firstly, most disorders lattice-networks consider the random removal of bonds within the lattice. 
Commonly, this is implemented during the network's generation by including (or equivalently excluding) a bond with probability $1 - p$, where $p$ defines the desired fraction of bonds wanted in the lattice \cite{broedersz_criticality_2011,head_distinct_2003}. This is commonly done when looking at the role of percolation in these models \cite{broedersz_criticality_2011, feng_percolation_1984, das_redundancy_2012}. 

While simply removing bonds at random may not seem at first to introduce enough disorder in the system, it directly affects the rigidity of the system (as discussed later in \cref{sec:Rigidity}) with a distinct phase changes at rigidity percolation threshold $p_c$, where the network undergoes a transition from isolated rigid clusters to a single system-spanning one \cite{jacobs_generic_1995,zhang_correlated_2019} and at the isostatic point where the network loses its first order rigidity. The benefit of removing bonds from a lattice-based network is also the breakup of long system-spanning force chains \cite{ruiz-franco_force_2022}, which naturally occur as a result of the network's construction. Networks which do not undergo this pruning will generally present an initial low-strain softening of the shear modulus $K$ \cite{licup_nonlinear_2016} associated with the buckling of the long fibres. 

The effects of these system-spanning force chains can be mitigated by the second type of disorder commonly used in discorded lattice networks, where the natural positions of the network's nodes are perturbed and the bonds' natural lengths and angles redefined \cite{jacobs_generic_1995, krushynska_multi-frequency_2025, sheinman_nonlinear_2012, head_predicting_2025}. A network which has this kind of disorder is often referred to as a generic network \cite{jacobs_generic_1995}. The size of these prelibations is often constrained to less than half the minimum lattice spacing $\delta_{min} / 2$ to stop overlapping nodes \cite{shivers_normal_2019}. 

\subsubsection{Packing-Based Networks} \label{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}

Packing-based networks, as the name implies, are conducted from the packing configurations of soft spheres and have been extensively studied to study elastic or fibre networks \cite{rens_rigidity_2019,wyart_elasticity_2008,lerner_unified_2012,shivers_normal_2019,shivers_scaling_2019,shivers_phase_2022}. Jammed networks are generated through minimising the total energy of a bidispersed ensemble of disks with at density above the jamming transition $\phi_J$ \cite{Hernjamming2003,BoyerJamming2011,ikeda_unified_2012}. Each disk is initially placed at a random position within a square domain of size $L_x=L_y=L$, with radius $R_0$ or $f R_0$ in equal proportion. The value $f$ is chosen such that it reduces the level of crystallisation in the system; a commonly used value, and the one adopted in this thesis, is $f=1.4$. This choice yields packings which exhibit little long-range macroscopic order\cite{van_hecke_jamming_2010, chacko_slow_2019, desmond_random_2009, koezeMappingJammingTransition2016}. The value $R0$ is scaled such that the target density $\phi$ is achieved in the box. 

While there have been several forms of soft disk potentials used, we will consider the potential used in Ref. \cite{chacko_slow_2019}. In this work, overlapping disks interact through a pairwise repulsive interaction potential of the form
\begin{equation}
    U_{ij}\left(r_{ij}\right)=R_0^3\left(1-\frac{r_{ij}}{D_{ij}}\right)^2\Theta(D_{ij}-r_{ij})
\end{equation}
where $r_{ij}$ denotes the distance between particles $i$ and $j$, $D_{ij}=R_i+R_j$ is the sum of the two particles' radii and $\Theta(x)$ is the Heaviside function (defined in \cref{symbol:heaviside_function}). The total interaction energy is obtained by summing over all distinct particle pairs 
\begin{equation*}
    U_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{i}\sum_{j<i} U_{ij}.
\end{equation*}
In Ref. \cite{chacko_slow_2019}, this is minimised by integrating an overdamped equation of motion (equivalent to the method of steepest descent), as the focus is on the relaxation dynamics, rather than solely on the final minimised configuration. In our network generation, we use the Fast Inertial Relaxation Engine (Fire) algorithm \cite{bitzek_structural_2006, guenole_assessment_2020}, which can reach a minimum energy state more efficiently, but does not capture physically accurate relaxation dynamics.

\subsubsection{Pruning Networks}
During network creation, networks can be left with disconnected or dangling bond clusters defined in \cref{fig:PruningNetworks}. These do not contribute to the network's mechanical response in the dynamics we will use in this thesis, and as such are removed before simulations. Disconnected clusters, shown in \cref{fig:PruningNetworks}a, are removed by identifying all connected components in the networks and removing all but the largest, corresponding to the bulk network. Correspondingly dangling clusters, shown in \cref{fig:PruningNetworks}b, are connected to the bulk network by a single bond. In graph theory, this cluster is known as a Biconnected component, and the connecting bond a Bridge. We utilise the NetworkX Python package \cite{SciPyProceedings_11} to quickly identify the connected and biconnected components.

\subsection{Calculating Stress} \label{sec:viralStress}

In molecular dynamics simulations, defining stress precisely can be challenging or computationally demanding. While methods such as the principle of virtual work allow for the system's stress to be found by taking a numerical derivative of the Hamiltonian of the system. However, these methods are computationally expensive as evaluating the Hamiltonian requires the relaxation of the system after the small strain step $\delta\gamma$/$\delta\epsilon$ and possibly averaging over several incremental shear strain steps to compute the derivative accurately. Instead, stresses reported by molecular dynamical systems report the viral stress of the system. While there are many formulations of the viral stress tensor \cite{walton_pressure_1983,erpenbeck_einstein-kubo-helfand_1995,tsai_virial_1979}, we will present a brief derivation of the viral stress tensor derived by Irving and Kirkwood in Ref. \cite{irving_statistical_1950} in two dimensions.

Following the arguments of Doi and Edwards in Ref. \cite{doi_theory_1988}, we consider an arbitrary line perpendicular to the $j$-axis, which intersects the network at a position $h$ along the $j$-axis. The $ij$ stress component on this line, $\sigma_{ij}(h)$, is by definition the $\alpha$ component of the force (per length) exerted by the section of the network above the plane onto the section network below the plane, denoted $F_i(h)$. The macroscopic stress $\sigma_{ij}$ exerted by the network can thus be computed by averaging $\sigma_{ij}(h)$ over the full length of the network along the $j$-axis,
\begin{gather}
    \sigma_{ij} = \left< \sigma_{ij}(h)\right> = \left< F_i(h)\right> / L_i \\
    \sigma_{ij} = \frac{1}{L_iL_j}\int_0^L F_i(h) dh \label{eq:viralDevInt}
\end{gather}
where $L_i$ and $L_j$ is the length of the network intersected by the line and is the full length of the intersection along the $j$-axis, respectively. We note that $L_iL_j$ is the area of our system $A$. For simplicity, we ignore the solvent effect and consider only the contribution to the stress from the inter-node forces. 
The force $F_i(h)$ can be expressed as the sum of all the $i$-component of the force $f_{mn}$ exserted on node $m$ by node $n$
\begin{equation}
    F_i(h) = \sum_{m,n} f_{mn,i} \Theta(h-r_{n,j})\Theta(r_{m,j}-h)
    \label{eq:viralDevForce}
\end{equation}
where $r_{n,j}$ denotes the position of node $n$ along the $j$-axis. The two $\Theta$ functions (defined in \cref{symbol:heaviside_function}) restrict node $n$ to be below the line and node $m$ to above the line. Substituting \cref{eq:viralDevForce} into $\cref{eq:viralDevInt}$ and integrating over $h$ gives
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{ij} &= \frac{1}{A} \sum_{m,n} f_{mn,i}(r_{n,j}-r_{n,j})\Theta(r_{n,j}-r_{m,j}) \label{eq:viralDevTheta} \\
    \sigma_{ij} &= \frac{1}{2A} \sum_{m,n} f_{mn,i}(r_{n,j}-r_{m,j}) \label{eq:viralDevfinal} \\
    \sigma_{ij} &= \frac{1}{2A} \sum_{m,n} f_{mn,i}r_{mn,j} \label{eq:viralStress}
\end{align}
where $\bm{r}_{mn} = \bm{r}_{n}-\bm{r}_{m}$. We note that $\Theta$ in \cref{eq:viralDevTheta} restricts the position of node $n$ to be higher than node $m$ along the $j$-axis. By Newton's second law $\bm{f}_{mn} = -\bm{f}_{nm}$ and symmetry under exchanging $m$ and $n$, we see that it can be replaced by a factor of $1/2$ to instead correct for double counting in the summation.

The derivation above extends trivially to three dimensions, where we instead consider the force contribution from above and below an arbitrary plane perpendicular to the $j$-axis. Calculating the stress by this approach eliminates the computation of numerical derivatives and is more computationally efficient, as both $\bm{r}_{ij}$ and $\bm{f}_{ij}$ are already calculated at each timestep when evaluating the system's dynamics. A more thorough derivation of the viral stress tensor, including fluid effects, can be found in Ref. \cite{yang_generalized_2012}.

\subsection{Generalised Lees-Edwards Boundary Conditions} \label{sec:Lees-Edwards}

When studying rheology, we typically wish to apply a macroscopic strain to a material and study the resulting macroscopic stress. In an experimental setup, this involves placing a sample of the material of interest into a rheometer and applying a macroscopic strain by moving one or more of the sample's boundaries. To measure the material's bulk properties, the samples often need to be large to avoid edge effects dominating the physics. This proves a challenge in simulations where achieving these scales is computationally expensive or impossible. A common way to avoid these issues is to use periodic boundary conditions, where an object passing through one side of the unit cell reappears on the opposite side with the same velocity. This, by definition, removes the edges of the computational systems; therefore, macroscopic strains must be applied through an alternative method, rather than being applied the conventional way, i.e. by applying a stress along one of the edges.

Theoretically, macroscopic stresses are instead applied by deforming the lattice vectors, which define the relationship between the unit cell and its periodic images. Applying simple stresses to the system can therefore be trivially applied simply by deforming the unit cell and lattice vectors of the system, as shown in \cref{fig:Tri-Clinic}. Deforming the box in this manner is often referred to as triclinic boundary conditions, with the name derived from triclinic crystal structure. While this method allows for arbitrary deformation to be applied, it can be computationally expensive to apply due to the need to convert between bases when calculating minimum images and struggles at large strains where the unit cell is heavily deformed. Instead, a set of suitably modified periodic boundaries was proposed by Lees and Edwards in \cite{lees_computer_1972} to be computationally similar. To apply shear in the x-direction using Lees-Edwards boundary conditions, the particles' positions $\mathbf{r}_i = (x_i,y_i)$ are affinely transformed as 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}x_i \\ y_i
    \end{pmatrix} \to 
    \begin{pmatrix}x_i + \gamma y_i \\ y_i
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
and \quotes{sliding} the periodic images by a distance $\delta\gamma L_y$ in the adjacent rows as shown in \cref{fig:Lees-Edwards}. This proves more computationally efficient as the unit cell remains orthogonal. The computation details for implementing these can be found in Ref.\cite{allen_computer_2017}.
