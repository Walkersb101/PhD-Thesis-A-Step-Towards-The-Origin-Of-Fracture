\chapter{Ultradelayed Material Failure via Shear Banding After Straining in Networks} \label{sec:Ultradelayed}

\section{Introduction}
In this chapter, we investigate the behaviour of network materials subjected to the step strain protocol outlined in \cref{sec:DeformationProtocols}. In particular, we focus on the occurrence of delayed failure a long time after the initial imposition of strain. 

This work is motivated by previous studies of the same phenomenon by Lockwood \textit{et al.} \cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}. Using a variety of constitutive models, this work explores the formation of delayed dramatic shear banding instabilities long after the initial imposition of the strain. By introducing thermal effects into the simple network models described in \cref{sec:Networks}, we demonstrate how this phenomenon can arise in a more realistic model.

The phenomenon of interest in this work is the formation of shear bands or fractures long after the initial deformation. Shear banding and failure have been widely explored both experimentally and theoretically, as discussed in \cref{sec:ShearBanding}, and have been shown to show failure in a range of protocols. Typically, protocols such as shear startup, outlined in \cref{sec:DeformationProtocols}, where at some time $t=0$ the system is subjected to a constant shear rate $\dot{\gamma}$, have been used to study the formation of these bands \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,pollard_yielding_2022,moorcroft_age-dependent_2011,divouxTransientShearBanding2010,divoux_stress_2011,kurokawa_avalanche-like_2015}. Here shear stress $\Sigma_{xy}$ accumulates roughly linearly with the applied strain $\gamma = \dot{\gamma}t$, until it reaches a maximum value where yielding initiates and the stress decays to a flowing steady state. 

Alternatively, the material is subjected to a fixed stress of amplitude $\Sigma$ for $t \geq 0$ (a stress-controlled regime). Here, the material relaxes slowly with the material's strain $\gamma$ creeping forward with a rate that decays as $\dot{\gamma} \sim t^{-\alpha}$, with $\alpha \in \left( 0,1 \right)$, before suddenly failing as the material yield \cite{miguel_dislocation_2002,gibaud_heterogeneous_2010,leocmach_creep_2014,ballesta_creep_2016,liuCreepDynamicsAthermal2018,cabriolu_precursors_2019,siebenburger_creep_2012}.

In both cases, damage in the material accumulates over time either by increasing strain triggering yielding events or through stress propagation originating from a previous yielding event. Although during the initial stages of deformation, the shear response of the material is often macroscopically uniform, as this damage accumulates, this homogeneous state typically breaks down and deformation becomes spatially heterogeneous. This shear localisation eventually passes a critical point, and the flow reorganises into shear bands with contrasting strain rates, separated by interfaces aligned along the shear gradient direction. This effect has been shown theoretically within a minimal set of continuum rheological constitutive assumptions \cite{fielding_shear_2014,moorcroft_age-dependent_2011,moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_transient_2011,fielding_triggers_2016}

Here instead, we consider a step strain protocol in which, at time $t=0$, the material is rapidly deformed to a finite fixed shear strain $\gamma_0$, which is then held constant thereafter. Unlike the shear startup and creep protocols, in which the strain increases indefinitely, and as such can support a final fluidised state, it has been assumed that the relaxation process of these materials is predictable. The material's stress is assumed to either decay to a constant value or catastrophically decrease as the material fails, depending on the magnitude of the imposed strain $\gamma_0$. A schematic illustration of these two responses is shown in \cref{fig:trivialRelaxation}. 

In the work done by Lockwood, it is shown that the material may suffer a dramatic internal shear
banding instability long after the imposed deformation\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}. Importantly, this time can be delayed arbitrarily with little to no change to the macroscopic observables in the material. Therefore, an observer who lacks any knowledge of the materials' history could be caught entirely unawares by the instability.

As the step strain protocol is less commonly studied compared to others, little work exists before the work by Lockwood \textit{et al.}. Some alternative theoretical studies looked at the formation of banding over short timescales as the material fails immediately \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_nonmonotonic_2009}. And experimentally in polymer melts, the same short timescale failures have been observed \cite{boukany_exploring_2009,boukany_step_2009,barroso_evaluation_2002,einaga_stress_1973}. Experiments examining the longer timescale dynamics after the system appears to have reached equilibrium are even less explored, although some studies have shown signs of delayed yielding similar to what is presented in Ref. \cite {lockwood_ultradelayed_2025} and to the results we present later \cite{boukany_step_2009,jacob_rheological_2019,van_der_kooij_laser_2018}.

In contrast, the step strain protocol has previously been studied in the context of unbreakable fibre networks by Shivers et al. in Ref.~\cite{shivers_strain-controlled_2023}. We reproduce the relevant features of this behaviour in \cref{fig:networkRelaxation}. Although their work includes a detailed analysis of relaxation timescales and dynamics, these aspects are not central to the focus of this chapter. Instead, the key feature for our purposes is the strain-stiffening point, which exists for networks with connectivities $z<4$. Below this point, the network is naturally floppy and possesses many zero-energy modes. Beyond a critical strain $\gamma_c(z)$, these modes are eliminated, and the network is able to sustain stress in a manner comparable to networks above the isostatic point ($z>4$). We examine the origins of this transition in more detail in \cref{sec:Rigidity}.

This work aims to explore the delayed failure of these network materials by introducing a thermal breakage condition to the bond fibres. For computational efficiency, we will use a method analogous to the yielding condition found in the Soft Glassy Rheology (SGR) model \cite{sollich_rheological_1998,sollich_thermodynamic_2012} and prescribe a yielding rate to each bond in the network. Under this construction, we will explore how the failure of these networks can be tuned so that the failure of the material can be almost indefinitely delayed. In the remainder of this chapter, we will introduce the construction of the failure rate $\Gamma$, use the model to explore this delayed failure phenomenon at high and low strain, and characterise the effect of perturbing both strain and temperature in these regimes.

\section{Rate Dependent Fracture} \label{sec:BoltzmanFracture}
To explore the ultra-delayed failure of networks, we extend the network model outlined in \cref{sec:Networks}, specifically focusing on the packing-derived networks defined in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}. It is common when exploring fractures in mesoscopic network models to introduce a finite breakage strain $\lambda$, where bonds with axial strain $\epsilon_b=(l-l_0)/l_0 \geq \lambda$ fail \cite{tauber_stretchy_2022}. While this model could be used to understand an athermal fracture in a step strain regime, it is unable to resolve the temperature dependence needed for delayed yielding. 

Taking inspiration from the Soft Glassy Rheology (SGR) model \cite{sollich_rheological_1998,sollich_thermodynamic_2012}. We will consider each bond to sit in its own local energy well of depth $E$. Each bond then has a local strain energy $E_b = \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2$ where $\epsilon_b = (l-l_0)/l_0$ is the axial strain on that bond. Each bond is therefore allowed to explore, via thermal fluctuations, the energy well, defined by its strain energy $E_b$ and failure energy $E$. These thermal fluctuations can be thought of as arising from both the surrounding material and the bond's internal structure. Small fluctuation from the equilibrium point are therefore more common, while large excursions away from the equilibrium point are increasingly less common due to the higher energy costs.

As the local strain energy $E_b$ increases towards $E$, the extra energy required from thermal fluctuation decreases exponentially and therefore the probability of escaping the well increases. This idea leads to an Arrhenius distribution for the rate of a bond failing
\begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\epsilon_b) \sim \exp \left[ -\dfrac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2}{x} \right],
\end{equation*}
where $x$ is an effective temperature, controlling the amplitude of the allowed fluctuations. In systems where the thermal energy is of the same order as the depth of the energy well, the temperature parameter can be interpreted as the true thermodynamic temperature, with $x = k_B T$ \cite{sollich_thermodynamic_2012}. However, as in the SGR model, the energy depths in our network are much larger than the thermal contributions, the effective noise temperature is more closely related to how stress would propagate across the microscopic structure of the bond.

We will also adopt a slight variation to this form, where an upper rate is placed on the rate of failing \cite{sollich_thermodynamic_2012},
\begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\epsilon_b) \sim \begin{cases}
        \exp \left[ -\dfrac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2}{x} \right] & \text{if } E_b < E \\
        1 & \text{if } E_b \geq E.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
This decouples the rate of failing from the bond's strain after the bond has escaped the well, with the probability remaining constant rather than rising indefinitely. This also has the additional benefit of increasing the numerical stability of the system, as an infinitely small time step is no longer required to accurately resolve the rate $\Gamma$. 

To implement this explicitly, we introduce a microscopic rate $\Gamma_0$ that sets the characteristic attempt frequency for bond failure. The failure rate of an individual bond is then given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:SGRRate}
    \Gamma(\epsilon_b) = \Gamma_0 \begin{cases}
        \exp \left[ -\dfrac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2}{x} \right] & \text{if } E_b < E \\
        1 & \text{if } E_b \geq E.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The factor $\Gamma_0$ corresponds to the microscopic hopping rate of the bonds, for which we will work in units where $\Gamma_0=1$. We illustrate an example of $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$ in \cref{fig:FailureRate} for a bond with $E=1.0$ and $x=0.1$.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/Figures/Rate.tikz}
    %\missingfigure{An illustration of the rate failure rate $\Gamma$ of a bond as a function of bond strain $\epsilon_b$}
    \caption[An illustration of the rate failure rate $\Gamma$ of a bond as a function of bond strain $\epsilon_b$.]{An illustration of the rate failure rate $\Gamma$ of a bond as a function of bond strain $\epsilon_b$. Plotted for $E=1.0$ and $x=0.1$}
    \label{fig:FailureRate}
\end{figure}

Bond failure is assumed to occur as a Poisson process with rate $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$. At the end of each discrete time step $\delta t$, each bond undergoes a Bernoulli trial with failure probability
\begin{equation*}
    p(E_b) = 1 - \exp\left[ -\Gamma(\epsilon_b)\delta t \right].
\end{equation*}
This construction preserves the correct continuous-time Poisson limit while remaining numerically stable for finite time steps. 

This choice in breaking condition gives two free variables, $E$ and $x$, for each given $\gamma_0$. The parameters used in the following case studies were found through parameter sweeps of phase space, with starting points found using the method described in \cref{sec:approxEx}.

\section{Stress Decay and Response} \label{sec:lowStrainResponse}

The step‐strain protocol, in which a strain of amplitude $\gamma_0$ is instantaneously applied and then held constant, was simulated using the dynamics described in \cref{sec:NetworkDynamics}, together with the fracture criteria outlined in \cref{sec:BoltzmanFracture}. Results for a fixed strain amplitude $\gamma_0 = 0.01$ and a set of temperatures spaced logarithmically in the range $x \in [2\times10^{-7}, 2\times10^{-6}]$ are shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering

    \caption[Normalised stress decay $\sigma/\sigma_0$ as a function of time $t$ following a step strain $\gamma_0$]{Normalised stress decay $\sigma/\sigma_0$ as a function of time $t$ following the imposition of a step strain of amplitude $\gamma_0=0.1$, with breakage parameter $E=5.025\times10^{-5}$. \textbf{Inset:} the same data plotted on a linear scale.}
    \label{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}
\end{figure}

We measure the stress decay $\sigma(t)$ relative to the stress immediately after the imposed step strain, $\sigma_0$. At $t=0$, the stress is given by the linear elastic response $\sigma_0 = G_{xy}\gamma_0$, where $G_{xy}$ is the shear modulus of the network. This modulus depends on the network topology and the bond stiffness $\mu$, and has been extensively characterised by Shivers \textit{et al.} in Ref.~\cite{shivers_scaling_2019}. In what follows, we focus on the relaxation dynamics relative to this initial value $\sigma_0$ rather than the absolute value of $\sigma$.

At short times, the stress relaxation is nearly identical across all temperatures, reflecting the initial stage of non-affine network rearrangement. At later times, the relaxation curves diverge as networks undergo failure, which is accompanied by a pronounced, temperature-dependent drop in stress.

For low temperatures, the early-time relaxation closely resembles that shown in \cref{fig:networkRelaxation}, with the stress reaching an extended plateau at an approximately constant value. Although these networks ultimately fail in a brittle manner at times $t \gg 0$, we observe no detectable precursor in the stress response before failure. While the failure appears instantaneous in the main figure due to the scale of the plot, the inset resolves a smaller time window, revealing qualitatively similar failure behaviour across the failure regime, albeit still characterised by an abrupt onset.

In previous work on delayed failure in elastoplastic models \cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}, it is common to look for precursors to shear banding through the displacement profile. Instead, we consider an analogous measure and consider the number of bonds broken in the network $B$. This directly measures the amount of plasticity which has occurred in the system, although it lacks any spatial information.

We plot the corresponding bond-breakage response curves in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempBondResponse}, showing the number of bonds that have broken during the strain response. These curves display behaviour consistent with \cref{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}: they diverge at early times, followed by a rapid increase in bond failure that coincides with the sharp stress drops at the point of macroscopic failure.

To gain further insight into the failure mechanisms of each network, we present representative snapshots of the microstructure at both low and high temperature in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}. We begin by considering the high-temperature cases, shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}b. In this regime, failure is characterised by extensive diffuse damage: bond breakage occurs throughout the system in a largely spatially uncorrelated manner. As a result, the network rapidly loses rigidity, which is reflected in the corresponding drop in stress.

By contrast, at lower temperatures the networks predominantly fail through a highly localised rupture event. Here, the initial failure of a single bond redistributes stress to its neighbours, sufficiently loading them to bring them close to their own failure thresholds. This initiates a cascade of correlated bond breakage events that propagates through the material, ultimately culminating in the macroscopic failure of the network.

In this small-strain regime, we observe that macroscopic failure tends to propagate either vertically or horizontally with approximately equal probability when viewed in the rest frame of the network. We attribute this to the fact that, at sufficiently small strains, the network remains largely homogeneous and approximately isotropic. Consequently, the stress redistribution induced by a single bond failure is well described by the Eshelby stress propagator, as discussed in \cref{sec:EshelbyPropogator}. This predicts that bonds aligned vertically and horizontally with the failed bond experience an increase in stress, whereas diagonally adjacent bonds are unloaded.

\section{Banding Time $t^*$ and Temperature $x$} \label{sec:LowGammaTemp}

To quantify the delay in fracture following the imposition of the step strain, we define and track a characteristic failure time $t^*$ for the material. For simplicity, we take $t^*$ to be the time at which the stress first falls below $75\%$ of its initial value $\sigma_0$. This threshold is indicated by the red line in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}. Importantly, this criterion lies below the stress plateau established immediately after the step strain, and therefore can only be crossed once the network has undergone significant bond failure. The resulting failure times are plotted in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}a.

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/LowGammaTemp/FailureJoin.pdf}
    \caption[Low-strain failure time and bond breaks versus temperature]{Failure time $t^*$ and the corresponding number of broken bonds $B^*$ as a function of temperature $x$ at low strain ($\gamma_0=0.01$). \newline
    Parameters: $E=5.025\times10^{-5}$.}
    \label{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}
\end{figure}

In \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}a we see an initial regime where the failure time of the material increases rapidly with decreasing temperature. At these low temperatures, the rate of stress decay is driven by a loss of bonds, rather than the relaxation of the network resulting from non-affine rearrangements. This can be seen in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}b where the number of bonds at failure $B^*$, decays rapidly with increasing temperature. As each bond fails, the system loses some stress, which locally may be partially recovered as the system rearranges itself to restore force balance within the material. However, the rate at which bonds fail early in the response is high enough that the material is unable to recover. 

We next consider a temperature low enough that the corresponding rates of failure $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$, determined by \cref{eq:SGRRate}, are a lot greater than the characteristic relaxation time of the material. In this regime, bonds do not typically fail during the initial relaxation, stopping diffuse damage from occurring early in the response. As such, the network can relax into an equilibrium state, where the resultant physics becomes dependent on the failure of the most strained bond. 

In this configuration, all bonds have relaxed to their equilibrium positions under the imposed boundary conditions. Consequently, each bond has a well-defined and constant failure rate $\Gamma$ determined by its rest strain. The system remains in this state until the next bond failure occurs, which triggers a local rearrangement of the surrounding network.

At present, we cannot definitively identify the mechanism that determines the outcome of an individual bond failure. In some instances, the failure of a single bond results only in a small, localised deformation that does not precipitate further damage. In other cases, however, a single bond failure can act as a nucleus for brittle fracture, initiating a propagating crack that ultimately leads to an almost complete loss of bulk stress. Signatures of this intermittent behaviour are evident in the step-like features observed in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempBondResponse} for all low-temperature cases. Prior to the final catastrophic fracture event, isolated bond failures or small clusters of failures may occur and persist for extended periods before the next failure is triggered.

This uncertainty leads to a large variance in the measured failure times. The outcome becomes increasingly sensitive to the underlying network topology, such that some networks fail immediately after the first bond breaks, whereas others can sustain multiple isolated failures before ultimately fracturing. This variability is reflected in the large error bars shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}a; on the log–log scale, a single error bar spans nearly the entire region beneath the mean curve. This is to be expected from a Poisson process, where the variance, mean and rate are all equal.

The lower bound on the failure time of these systems is ultimately set by the rate defined in \cref{eq:SGRRate} for the given parameters $x$ and $E$, which prescribes the average time for the first bond, and subsequent uncorrelated bonds, to break. Network topology can further delay catastrophic failure, as the material must wait for a bond with a sufficiently susceptible local environment to fail and nucleate brittle fracture. As discussed in \cref{sec:Rigidity}, predicting local deformations in this problem is provably very challenging; consequently, it is not possible to identify these critical bonds from the initial configuration alone. The system is therefore well defined at the ensemble level, yet highly unpredictable for any individual realisation.

\section{Banding Time and the Imposed Strain $\gamma_0$} \label{sec:DelayedGamma}

When considering the variations of temperature $x$ in \cref{sec:LowGammaTemp}, we were able to control the rate at which bonds failed by controlling the steepness of each bond's energy well. As the same strain $\gamma_0=0.01$ is applied to each network, the bonds, for the moment ignoring failures, relax to identical configurations only depending on the network structure rather than any macroscopically applied parameter. As a result, the failure times of low-temperature networks, which experience little failure early in the relaxation, depend only on the noise temperature $x$ and the stochastic nature of the underlying Poisson failure process.

Under differing imposed step strains $\gamma_0$, each network relaxes into a slightly different configuration. Consequently, subtly distinct subsets of bonds are positioned close to the edges of their energy wells. Despite these microscopic differences, the macroscopic response of the system and the associated banding times $t^*$, when examined as a function of $\gamma_0$, exhibit the same qualitative behaviour as that reported in \cref{sec:LowGammaTemp}. We report the resulting failure times, and the corresponding number of bond breaks $B^*$ at failure, in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainValues}a and b, respectively. We fix the temperature $x$ to be $10^{-6}$ and vary the step strain amplitude in the range $\gamma_0\in[0.009, 0.0011]$.

The dependence of the failure time on the imposed step strain follows a comparatively simpler and more systematic trend across the explored parameter range, with $t^*$ increasing approximately exponentially. The underlying failure physics again transitions from diffuse to brittle behaviour with increasing strain; however, even at the highest strains considered, failure proceeds through a series of small cracks rather than a single system-spanning rupture, as illustrated in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainSnapshots}b. In these higher-strain cases, the initial step strain brings multiple bonds close to their failure threshold, leading to early, spatially distributed breakage events, as seen in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainResponse}b. Each of these initial failures subsequently develops into its own macroscopic crack, whose ultimate size is limited by the total stress stored in the system.

As illustrated in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainResponse}a, the overall stress responses appear consistent across varying both strain and temperature in these low strain cases, with delayed yielding occurring in highly brittle cases where single bond failures can cascade and result in a macroscopic failure of the networks. In these cases, either at low temperature or low strain, physics plays out identically, with the system waiting for a critical bond to fail and nucleate a macroscopic fracture, resulting in almost identical failures as shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}a and \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainSnapshots}a. Both cases are results of the same bond failing late into the response, resulting in similar but not identical crack failure.  

As illustrated in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainResponse}a, the overall stress responses remain qualitatively consistent across variations in both strain and temperature in these low-strain cases. In highly brittle regimes, delayed yielding arises when individual bond failures cascade and ultimately trigger a macroscopic fracture of the network. In the limits of either low temperature or low strain, the underlying physics is effectively the same: the system remains intact until a critical bond fails, which nucleates a macroscopic crack. This leads to nearly indistinguishable failure morphologies, as shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}a and \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainSnapshots}a. In both cases, the macroscopic fracture originates from a single bond that fails late in the response, producing similar, though not identical, failure patterns.

So far, we have focused on regimes in which the imposed step strain $\gamma_0$ is small compared with the characteristic strain scale of the network. We now consider the response of the system under large imposed strains, $\gamma_0 \sim 1$, where the deformation is sufficient to induce substantial non-affine rearrangements within the network \cite{shivers_scaling_2019}. We therefore expect qualitatively different relaxation and failure behaviour in this regime. In what follows, we present results obtained using $E = 0.35$, temperature $x = 0.0062$, and step strains in the range $\gamma_0 \in [0.9,1.0]$.

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering

In \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainStress}a we observe this non-affine rearrangement as the networks relax to different levels of normalised stress, which increase with decreasing strain amplitude. In this regime, delayed failure again emerges, with the failure time $t^*$
 following an approximately exponential dependence as shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainStress}a. This trend closely resembles that in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainValues}a, with the failure time increasing without bound for all imposed step strains considered. Notably, the standard deviations increase with decreasing strain, consistent with failure becoming increasingly governed by a Poissonian bond-breaking process.

The total number of bond failures increases substantially, rising from approximately 800 to 1800 as $\gamma_0$ is increased from $0.9$ to $1.0$. However, this increase is smooth and systematic, while at smaller $\gamma_0$ the failure count remains comparatively flat. This suggests that, in this high-strain regime, the extent of damage is still primarily controlled by the failure rate rather than by changes in network topology. Here, the systems still wait for a critical bond to fail, allowing a macroscopic crack to form.

The main deviation from this overall trend occurs at very early times, where all systems exhibit a pronounced burst of bond failures associated with diffuse background activity prior to the network reaching a steady, relaxed configuration. This early activity appears as spatially scattered bond breakage, as shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}, and is characteristic of the high-strain response. These failures appear to form across the whole parameter range; however, increasing in amount with increasing strain amplitude as more bonds are brought closer to their failure threshold $E$. 

At higher strains, shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}a, failure for a fixed breakage threshold $E$ proceeds more diffusely. It occurs through a series of smaller diffuse cracks rather than a single brittle failure, as illustrated in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}b.

\section{Direction of fracture}

As discussed in \cref{sec:lowStrainResponse}, at small strain, the direction of crack propagation appears to be horizontal or vertical with roughly equal probability. Contrastingly, at high strain, as shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}, failure always propagates perpendicular to the principal strain axis.

This high-strain regime can be understood by considering the affine deformation of the bonds in the network. During deformation, bonds which lie parallel to the principal shear axis are elongated, whereas bonds perpendicular are contracted by a smaller amount.

\section{Calculating Approximate free parameters in Breakage Rate} \label{sec:approxEx}
Predicting the relaxed post-strain configurations of disordered networks is inherently challenging. This problem has been extensively studied in the context of rigidity (see \cref{sec:Rigidity}). Recent work exploring the high-dimensional energy landscapes of such networks has demonstrated that predicting their evolution is computationally intractable without direct numerical simulation \cite{damavandi_energetic_2022,damavandi_energetic_2022-1}. These difficulties arise primarily from the large non-affine rearrangements characteristic of semi-flexible networks. As the final parameter ranges will ultimately be refined through systematic parameter space searches, we require only a reasonable starting point rather than an exact prediction.


To obtain such an estimate, we first consider a single bond of rest length $l_0$ oriented at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal axis, with one node fixed at the origin. Under a simple shear deformation characterised by the matrix
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        1 & \gamma_0 \\
        0 & 1 \\
    \end{matrix}\right)
\end{equation*}
the initially free end of the bond, which starts at $(l_0\cos\theta,\, l_0\sin\theta)$, is transformed to
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        1 & \gamma_0 \\
        0 & 1 \\
    \end{matrix}\right)
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        l_0\cos(\theta) \\
        l_0\sin(\theta) \\
    \end{matrix}\right)
    =
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        l_0(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta)) \\
        l_0\sin(\theta) \\
    \end{matrix}\right).
\end{equation*}
The post-shear bond length is therefore
\begin{equation}
    l=l0\sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding bond strain is
\begin{align}
     \epsilon_b(\theta| \gamma_0) &= \frac{l_0\sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)} -l_0}{l_0} \\
     &= \sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)} - 1.
\end{align}
This expression for $\epsilon_b(\theta)$ neglects any non-affine rearrangements of the surrounding network and should therefore be interpreted as an approximate upper bound on the expected bond strains.


For the large, disordered networks considered here, we assume that bond orientations are approximately uniformly distributed. We therefore consider the maximum value of $\epsilon_b(\theta|\gamma_0)$ over $\theta \in (0,\pi)$ and define
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\max}(\gamma_0) = \max_{\theta \in (0,\pi)} \left\{ \epsilon_b(\theta|\gamma_0) \right\}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding maximum expected bond energy at strain $\gamma_0$ is then $\mu \epsilon_{\max}^2/2$.

As the networks we consider are large, we will assume that bonds start in a roughly uniform distribution of angles. We therefore consider the maximum values of $\epsilon_b(\theta | \gamma_0)$ in the range $\theta \in (0,\pi)$. We will refer to this as $\epsilon_{max}(\gamma_0) = \max\left\{ \epsilon_b(\theta | \gamma_0) : \theta \in (0,\pi) \right\}$. The maximum expected strain energy for a given strain $\gamma_0$ is therefore $\mu\epsilon_{max}^2/2$. We then solve for this at the centre of the chosen strain range and introduce a safety factor of two to account for the neglected non-affine rearrangements, which provides a reasonable starting point.

The corresponding maximum expected bond energy at strain $\gamma_0$ is then $\mu \epsilon_{\max}^2/2$. We estimate a suitable central value for the strain parameters by solving for this energy at the centre of the parameter range of interest and introducing a safety factor of approximately two to account for the neglected non-affine effects. This provides a practical starting point for our parameter exploration.


We adopt a similar strategy to obtain an approximate starting value for the temperature-like parameter $x$. Here we consider the mean bond energy, defined as
\begin{equation*}
    \langle \epsilon_b \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\mu}{2} \epsilon_b(\theta | \gamma_0)^2 d\theta.
\end{equation*}
Substituting this average strain into the failure rate given in \cref{eq:SGRRate} yields an average bond failure rate $\langle \Gamma \rangle$ for a typical bond in the system. Assuming that bond failures follow a Poisson process, such that the mean lifetime equals $1/\langle \Gamma \rangle$, we choose $x$ such that the average bond survives for a time $\langle \Gamma \rangle \approx 10^{20}$ in simulation units.

These estimates provide a reasonable initial parameter set. However, additional tuning is required to identify parameter combinations that best capture the relevant physics across the full range of behaviours studied in the main text.
