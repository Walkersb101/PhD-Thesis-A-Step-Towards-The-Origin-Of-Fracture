\chapter{Ultradelayed Material Failure via Shear Banding After Straining in Networks} \label{sec:Ultradelayed}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:delayedInto}
In this chapter, we investigate the behaviour of network materials subjected to the step strain protocol outlined in \cref{sec:DeformationProtocols}. In particular, we focus on the occurrence of delayed failure a long time after the initial imposition of strain. 

This work is motivated by previous studies of the same phenomenon by Lockwood \etal\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}, which demonstrated ultra-delayed shear banding instabilities using a variety of continuum constitutive models. While these results compellingly established that such behaviour is possible in principle, they did not address how this physics may emerge in a more realistic model. By introducing thermal bond-breaking effects into the simple network models described in \cref{sec:Networks}, we therefore demonstrate how this phenomenon can emerge from a more realistic, mesoscopic description of soft networks.

The phenomenon of interest in this work is the formation of shear bands or fractures long after the initial deformation. Shear banding and failure have been widely explored both experimentally and theoretically, as discussed in \cref{sec:ShearBanding}, and have been shown to show failure in a range of protocols. Typically, protocols such as shear startup, outlined in \cref{sec:DeformationProtocols}, where at some time $t=0$ the system is subjected to a constant shear rate $\dot{\gamma}$, have been used to study the formation of these bands \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,pollard_yielding_2022,moorcroft_age-dependent_2011,divouxTransientShearBanding2010,divoux_stress_2011,kurokawa_avalanche-like_2015}. Here shear stress $\Sigma_{xy}$ accumulates roughly linearly with the applied strain $\gamma = \dot{\gamma}t$, until it reaches a maximum value where yielding initiates and the stress decays to a flowing steady state. 

Alternatively, the material is subjected to a fixed stress of amplitude $\Sigma$ for $t \geq 0$ (a stress-controlled regime). Here, the material relaxes slowly with the material's strain $\gamma$ creeping forward with a rate that decays as $\dot{\gamma} \sim t^{-\alpha}$, with $\alpha \in \left( 0,1 \right)$, before suddenly decreasing as the material yield \cite{miguel_dislocation_2002,gibaud_heterogeneous_2010,leocmach_creep_2014,ballesta_creep_2016,liuCreepDynamicsAthermal2018,cabriolu_precursors_2019,siebenburger_creep_2012}.

In both cases, damage in the material accumulates over time either by increasing strain triggering yielding events or through stress propagation originating from a previous yielding event. Although during the initial stages of deformation, the shear response of the material is often macroscopically uniform, as the damage accumulates, this homogeneous state typically breaks down, and deformation becomes spatially heterogeneous. This shear localisation eventually passes a critical point, and the flow reorganises into shear bands with contrasting strain rates, separated by interfaces aligned along the shear gradient direction. This effect has been shown theoretically within a minimal set of continuum rheological constitutive assumptions \cite{fielding_shear_2014,moorcroft_age-dependent_2011,moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_transient_2011,fielding_triggers_2016}

Here, instead, we consider a step strain protocol in which, at time $t=0$, the material is rapidly deformed to a finite fixed shear strain $\gamma_0$, which is then held constant thereafter. Unlike the shear startup and creep protocols, in which the strain can increase indefinitely and can therefore sustain a fully fluidised steady state, the strain remains fixed in the present step strain protocol. Consequently, the subsequent relaxation processes of these materials have generally been assumed to be predictable. The material's stress is assumed to either decay slowly towards a finite value or catastrophically decrease as the material fails, depending on the magnitude of the imposed strain $\gamma_0$. A schematic illustration of these two responses is shown in \cref{fig:trivialRelaxation}.

This expectation is clearly justified in the athermal limit. In this regime, where thermal fluctuations are negligible and frictional forces dominate \cite{nicolas_deformation_2018,agoritsas_relevance_2015,ness_oscillatory_2017,lin_hydrodynamic_2015}, when a mesoscopic region of the material yields, the system is unable to redistribute the resulting strain without triggering a large avalanche of further yield events  \cite{barlow_ductile_2020}. Alternatively, once an athermal system has reached a relaxed (equilibrium) state under a fixed step strain, it is unable to relax further in any meaningful way. In the absence of thermal fluctuations, there is no mechanism available to drive additional microscopic rearrangements or further dissipation of stress beyond this point.

In athermal networks, the slow sequence of microscopic rearrangements that might 
otherwise build toward an eventual avalanche and produce delayed banding, which are therefore less likely than either rapid catastrophic failure or no failure at all. Although relaxation times have been shown to diverge at the strain stiffening point \cite{shivers_strain-controlled_2023}, hinting at a possible athermal mechanism for delayed yielding. We expect this behaviour to be highly network-dependent, and therefore will consider the effect in thermal systems, considering highly connected networks.

In the work done by Lockwood \etal, it is shown that in the thermal regime, materials may instead suffer a dramatic internal shear banding instability long after the imposed deformation\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}. Importantly, this time can be delayed arbitrarily with little to no change to the macroscopic observables in the material. Therefore, an observer who lacks any knowledge of the materials' history could be caught entirely unawares by the instability.

As the step strain protocol is less commonly studied compared to others, little work exists before the work by Lockwood \etal exploring this phenomenon. Some alternative theoretical studies looked at the formation of banding over short timescales as the material fails immediately \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_nonmonotonic_2009}. Experimentally, similar short-timescale failures have been observed in polymer melts \cite{boukany_exploring_2009,boukany_step_2009,barroso_evaluation_2002,einaga_stress_1973}. Experiments examining the longer timescale dynamics after the system appears to have reached equilibrium are even more limited. Although a small number of studies have reported signatures of delayed yielding qualitatively similar to those presented in Ref.~\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025} and to the results we present later in this chapter \cite{boukany_step_2009,jacob_rheological_2019,van_der_kooij_laser_2018}.

In contrast, the step strain protocol has previously been studied in the context of unbreakable fibre networks by Shivers \etal in Ref.~\cite{shivers_strain-controlled_2023}. We reproduce the relevant features of this behaviour in \cref{fig:networkRelaxation}. Although their work includes a detailed analysis of relaxation timescales and dynamics, these aspects are not central to the focus of this chapter. Instead, the key feature for our purpose is the strain-stiffening point, which exists for networks with connectivities $z<4$. Below this point, the network is naturally floppy and possesses many zero-energy modes. Beyond a critical strain $\gamma_c(z)$, these modes are eliminated, and the network can sustain stress in a manner comparable to networks above the isostatic point ($z>4$). We examine the origins of this transition in more detail in \cref{sec:Rigidity}.

This work aims to explore the delayed failure of these network materials by introducing a thermal breakage condition to the bond fibres. For computational efficiency, we will use a method analogous to the yielding condition found in the Soft Glassy Rheology (SGR) model \cite{sollich_rheological_1998,sollich_thermodynamic_2012} and prescribe a yielding rate to each bond in the network. Under this construction, we will explore how the failure of these networks can be tuned so that the failure of the material can be almost indefinitely delayed. In the remainder of this chapter, we will introduce the construction of the failure rate $\Gamma$, use the model to explore this delayed failure phenomenon at high and low strain, and characterise the effect of perturbing both strain and temperature in these regimes.

\section{Rate Dependent Fracture} \label{sec:BoltzmanFracture}
To explore the ultra-delayed failure of networks, we extend the network model outlined in \cref{sec:Networks}, specifically focusing on the packing-derived networks defined in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}. It is common when exploring fractures in mesoscopic network models to introduce a finite breakage strain $\lambda$, where bonds with axial strain $\epsilon_b=(l-l_0)/l_0 \geq \lambda$ fail \cite{tauber_stretchy_2022}. While this model could be used to understand athermal fracture in a step strain regime, it is unable to resolve the temperature dependence needed for delayed yielding.

Taking inspiration from the Soft Glassy Rheology (SGR) model \cite{sollich_rheological_1998,sollich_thermodynamic_2012}. We will consider each bond to sit in its own local energy well of depth $E$. Each bond then has a local strain energy $E_b = \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2$. Each bond is therefore allowed to explore, via thermal fluctuations, its own energy well, defined by its strain energy $E_b$ and failure energy $E$. These thermal fluctuations can be thought of as arising from both the surrounding material and the bond's internal structure. Small fluctuations from the equilibrium point are therefore more common, while large excursions away from the equilibrium point are increasingly less common due to the higher energy costs.

As the local strain energy $E_b$ increases towards $E$, the extra energy required from thermal fluctuations decreases exponentially and therefore the probability of escaping the well increases. This idea leads to an Arrhenius distribution for the rate of a bond failing
\begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\epsilon_b) \sim \exp \left[ -\dfrac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2}{x} \right],
\end{equation*}
where $x$ is an effective temperature, controlling the amplitude of the allowed fluctuations. In systems where the thermal energy is of the same order as the depth of the energy well, the temperature parameter can be interpreted as the true thermodynamic temperature, with $x = k_B T$ \cite{sollich_thermodynamic_2012}. However, as in the SGR model, the energy depths in our network are much larger than the thermal contributions, the effective noise temperature is more closely related to how stress would be distributed in the microscopic structure of the bond.

We will also adopt a slight variation to this form, where an upper rate is placed on the rate of failing \cite{sollich_thermodynamic_2012},
\begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\epsilon_b) \sim \begin{cases}
        \exp \left[ -\dfrac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2}{x} \right] & \text{if } E_b < E \\
        1 & \text{if } E_b \geq E.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
This decouples the rate of failing from the bond's strain after the bond has escaped the well, with the probability remaining constant rather than rising indefinitely. It also has the additional benefit of increasing the numerical stability of the system, as an infinitely small time step is no longer required to accurately resolve the rate $\Gamma$. 

To implement this explicitly, we introduce a microscopic rate $\Gamma_0$ that sets the characteristic attempt frequency for bond failure. The failure rate of an individual bond is then given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:SGRRate}
    \Gamma(\epsilon_b) = \Gamma_0 \begin{cases}
        \exp \left[ -\dfrac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2}{x} \right] & \text{if } E_b < E \\
        1 & \text{if } E_b \geq E.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The factor $\Gamma_0$ corresponds to the microscopic hopping rate of the bonds, for which we will work in units where $\Gamma_0=1$. We illustrate an example of $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$ in \cref{fig:FailureRate} for a bond with $E=1.0$ and $x=0.1$.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/Figures/Rate.tikz}
    %\missingfigure{An illustration of the rate failure rate $\Gamma$ of a bond as a function of bond strain $\epsilon_b$}
    \caption[Illustration of the rate failure rate of bonds $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$]{
    Bond failure rate $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$ as a function of bond strain $\epsilon_b$, shown for $E=1.0$ and $x=0.1$. The rate increases rapidly as bonds approach their failure threshold.}
    \label{fig:FailureRate}
\end{figure}

Bond failure is assumed to occur as a Poisson process with rate $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$. At the end of each discrete time step $\delta t$, each bond undergoes a Bernoulli trial with failure probability
\begin{equation*}
    p(E_b) = 1 - \exp\left[ -\Gamma(\epsilon_b)\delta t \right].
\end{equation*}
This construction preserves the correct continuous-time Poisson limit while remaining numerically stable for finite time steps. 

This choice in breaking condition gives two free variables, $E$ and $x$, for each given $\gamma_0$. The parameters used in the following case studies were found through parameter sweeps of phase space, with starting points found using the method described in \cref{sec:approxEx}.

\section{Stress Decay and Response} \label{sec:lowStrainResponse}

The step‐strain protocol, in which a strain of amplitude $\gamma_0$ is instantaneously applied and then held constant, was simulated using the dynamics described in \cref{sec:NetworkDynamics}, together with the fracture criteria outlined in \cref{sec:BoltzmanFracture}. Results for a fixed strain amplitude $\gamma_0 = 0.01$ and a set of temperatures spaced logarithmically in the range $x \in [2\times10^{-7}, 2\times10^{-6}]$ are shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}.

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/LowGammaTemp/Stress.pdf}
    \caption[Normalised stress decay $\sigma/\sigma_0$ as a function of time $t$ following a step strain $\gamma_0$]{Normalised stress decay $\sigma/\sigma_0$ as a function of time $t$ following the imposition of a step strain of amplitude $\gamma_0=0.01$, with breakage parameter $E=5.025\times10^{-5}$. \textbf{Inset:} shows the same data plotted on a linear scale.}
    \label{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}
\end{figure}

We measure the stress decay $\sigma(t)$ relative to the stress immediately after the imposed step strain, $\sigma_0$. At $t=0$, the stress is given by the linear elastic response $\sigma_0 = G_{xy}\gamma_0$, where $G_{xy}$ is the shear modulus of the network. This modulus depends on the network topology and the bond stiffness $\mu$, and has been extensively characterised by Shivers \etal in Ref.~\cite{shivers_scaling_2019}. In what follows, we focus on the relaxation dynamics relative to this initial value $\sigma_0$ rather than the absolute value of $\sigma$.

At short times, the stress relaxation is nearly identical across all temperatures, reflecting the initial stage of non-affine network rearrangement. At later times, the relaxation curves diverge as networks undergo failure, which is accompanied by a pronounced drop in stress.

For low temperatures, the early-time relaxation closely resembles that shown in \cref{fig:networkRelaxation}, with the stress reaching an extended plateau at an approximately constant value. Although these networks ultimately fail in a brittle manner at times $t \gg 0$, we observe no detectable precursor in the stress response before failure. While the failure appears instantaneous in the main figure due to the scale of the plot, the inset resolves a smaller time window, revealing qualitatively similar failure behaviour across the failure regime, albeit still characterised by an abrupt onset.

In previous work on delayed failure in elastoplastic models \cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}, it is common to look for precursors to shear banding through the displacement profile. Instead, we consider an alternative measure and consider the number of bonds broken in the network $B$. This directly measures the amount of plasticity which has occurred in the system, although it lacks any spatial information.

We plot the corresponding bond-breakage response curves in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempBondResponse}, showing the number of bonds that have broken during the strain response. These curves display behaviour consistent with \cref{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}: they diverge at early times, followed by a rapid increase in bond failure that coincides with the sharp stress drops at the point of macroscopic failure.

To gain further insight into the failure mechanisms of each network, we present representative snapshots of the microstructure at both low and high temperature in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}. We begin by considering the high-temperature cases, shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}b. In this regime, failure is characterised by extensive diffuse damage: bond breakage occurs throughout the system in a largely spatially uncorrelated manner. As a result, the network rapidly loses rigidity, which is reflected in the corresponding drop in stress. This has been seen in an alternative model by Tauber \etal in Ref.~\cite{tauber_role_2020}.

By contrast, at lower temperatures, the networks predominantly fail through a highly localised rupture event. Here, the initial failure of a single bond redistributes stress to its neighbours, sufficiently loading them to bring them close to their own failure thresholds. This initiates a cascade of correlated bond breakage events that propagates through the material, ultimately culminating in the macroscopic failure of the network.

In this small-strain regime, we observe that macroscopic failure tends to propagate either vertically or horizontally with approximately equal probability when viewed in the rest frame of the network. We attribute this to the fact that, at sufficiently small strains, the network remains largely homogeneous and isotropic. Consequently, the stress redistribution induced by a single bond failure is well described by the Eshelby stress propagator, as discussed in \cref{sec:EshelbyPropogator}. This predicts that bonds aligned vertically and horizontally with the failed bond experience an increase in stress, whereas diagonally adjacent bonds are unloaded.

\section{Banding Time $t^*$ and Temperature $x$} \label{sec:LowGammaTemp}

To quantify the delay in fracture following the imposition of the step strain, we define and track a characteristic failure time $t^*$ for the material. We take $t^*$ to be the time at which the stress first falls below $0.75\sigma_0$, as indicated by the red line in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempResponse}. This criterion lies below the stress plateau established immediately after the step strain, and therefore can only be crossed once the network has undergone significant bond failure. In practice, the trends reported below are robust to moderate changes in the chosen threshold, provided it is placed just below the post-relaxation plateau for all parameter sets considered. The resulting failure times are plotted in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}a.

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/LowGammaTemp/FailureJoin.pdf}
    \caption[Low-strain failure time and bond breaks versus temperature]{Failure time $t^*$ and the corresponding number of broken bonds $B^*$ as a function of temperature $x$ at low strain ($\gamma_0=0.01$). \newline
    Parameters: $E=5.025\times10^{-5}$.}
    \label{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}
\end{figure}

In \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}a we see an initial regime where the failure time of the material increases rapidly with decreasing temperature. At these low temperatures, the rate of stress decay is driven by a loss of bonds, rather than the relaxation of the network resulting from non-affine rearrangements. This can be seen in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}b where the number of bonds at failure $B^*$, decays rapidly with increasing temperature. As each bond fails, the system loses some stress, which locally may be partially recovered as the system rearranges itself to restore force balance within the material. However, the rate at which bonds fail early in the response is high enough that the material is unable to recover. 

We next consider a temperature low enough that the corresponding rates of failure $\Gamma(\epsilon_b)$, determined by \cref{eq:SGRRate}, are a lot greater than the characteristic relaxation time of the material. In this regime, bonds do not typically fail during the initial relaxation, stopping diffuse damage from occurring early in the response. As such, the network can relax into an equilibrium state, where the resultant physics becomes dependent on the failure of the most strained bond. 

As all systems are subjected to the same imposed shear strain $\gamma_0=0.01$, and the temperature is low enough so little damage occurs, each network relaxes to an identical configuration, only depending on the network structure rather than any macroscopically applied parameter. As a result, the failure times of a specific network realisation at low temperature, which experiences little failure early in the relaxation, depend only on the noise temperature $x$ and the stochastic nature of the underlying Poisson failure process. 

In this configuration, all bonds have relaxed to their equilibrium positions under the imposed boundary conditions. Consequently, each bond has a well-defined and constant failure rate $\Gamma$ determined by its rest strain. The system therefore remains in this state until the next bond failure occurs, which triggers a local rearrangement of the surrounding network.

At present, we cannot definitively identify the mechanism that determines the outcome of an individual bond failure. In some instances, the failure of a single bond results only in a small, localised deformation that does not precipitate further damage. In other cases, however, a single bond failure can act as a nucleus for brittle fracture, initiating a propagating crack that ultimately leads to an almost complete loss of bulk stress. Signatures of this behaviour are evident in the step-like features observed in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempBondResponse} for all low-temperature cases. Before the final catastrophic fracture event, isolated bond failures or small clusters of failures may occur and persist for extended periods before the next failure is triggered.

This uncertainty leads to a large variance in the measured failure times. The outcome becomes increasingly sensitive to the underlying network topology, such that some networks fail immediately after the first bond breaks, whereas others can sustain multiple isolated failures before ultimately fracturing. This variability is reflected in the large error bars shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempFailure}a; on the log–log scale, a single error bar spans nearly the entire region beneath the mean curve. This is to be expected from a Poisson process, where the mean time between events, variance, and inverse rate are all equal.

The lower bound on the failure time of these systems is ultimately set by the rate defined in \cref{eq:SGRRate} for the given parameters $x$ and $E$, which prescribes the average time for the first bond, and subsequent uncorrelated bonds, to break. Network topology can further delay catastrophic failure, as the material must wait for a bond with a sufficiently susceptible local environment to fail and nucleate brittle fracture. As discussed in \cref{sec:Rigidity}, predicting local deformations in this problem is computationally impractical without fully simulating the dynamics; consequently, it is not possible to identify these critical bonds from the initial configuration alone. The system is therefore well defined at the ensemble level, yet highly unpredictable for any individual realisation.

Under differing imposed step strains $\gamma_0$, each network relaxes into a slightly different configuration. Consequently, subtly distinct subsets of bonds are positioned close to the edges of their energy wells.

Despite these microscopic differences, the macroscopic response of the system and the associated banding times $t^*$, when examined as a function of $\gamma_0$, exhibit the same qualitative behaviour as that reported in \cref{sec:LowGammaTemp}. We report the resulting failure times, and the corresponding number of bond breaks $B^*$ at failure, in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainJoint}b and d, respectively. We fix the temperature $x$ to be $10^{-6}$ and vary the step strain amplitude in the range $\gamma_0\in[0.009, 0.011]$.

The dependence of the failure time on the imposed step strain follows a comparatively simpler and more systematic trend across the explored parameter range, with $t^*$ increasing approximately exponentially. The underlying failure physics again transitions from diffuse to brittle behaviour with increasing strain; however, even at the highest strains considered, failure proceeds through a series of small cracks rather than a single system-spanning rupture, as illustrated in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainSnapshots}b. In these higher-strain cases, the initial step strain brings multiple bonds close to their failure threshold, leading to early, spatially distributed breakage events. Each of these initial failures subsequently develops into its own macroscopic crack, whose ultimate size is limited by the total stress stored in the system.

In highly brittle regimes, delayed yielding arises when individual bond failures cascade and ultimately trigger a macroscopic fracture of the network. In the limits of either low temperature or low strain, the underlying physics is the same: the system remains intact until a critical bond fails, which nucleates a macroscopic crack. This leads to nearly indistinguishable patterns of failure, as shown in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempSnapShots}a and \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainSnapshots}a. In both cases, the macroscopic fracture originates from a single bond that fails late in the response, producing similar, though not identical, failure patterns.

\subsection{High Strain Regime}

So far, we have focused on regimes in which the imposed step strain $\gamma_0$ is small compared with the characteristic strain scale of the network. We now consider the response of the system under large imposed strains, $\gamma_0 \sim 1$, where the deformation is sufficient to induce substantial non-affine rearrangements within the network \cite{shivers_scaling_2019}. We therefore expect qualitatively different relaxation and failure behaviour in this regime. In what follows, we present results obtained using $E = 0.35$, temperature $x = 0.0062$, and step strains in the range $\gamma_0 \in [0.9,1.0]$.

    {\bf a)} Normalised stress response $\sigma/\sigma_0$. \newline
    {\bf b)} Failure time $t^*$. \newline
    {\bf c)} Failed number of bonds $B$ as a function of time $t$. \newline
    {\bf d)} Number of broken bonds at failure $B^*$. \newline
    Parameters: $E=0.35$.
    }
    \label{fig:HighGammaStrain}
\end{figure}

While qualitatively the curves reported \cref{fig:HighGammaStrain}a-b appear similar to previously presented response curves, we note several key differences in how these networks fail. In \cref{fig:HighGammaStrain}a observe that the non-affine rearrangements present in the network correspond to subtly different levels of normalised stress, which increase with decreasing strain amplitude. This leads to higher-strain cases beginning to fail from slightly higher normalised strains. This is a direct effect of the strain stiffening within the material, which increases the network's shear module $G_{xy}$ non-linearly with strain \cite{shivers_scaling_2019}. Within the parameter range explored, the delayed failure time of the material still grows exponentially with decreasing $\gamma_0$. This trend closely resembles that in \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainJoint}b, with the failure time apparently increasing without bound for all imposed step strains considered.

A characteristic feature of failures at the high strains considered here is the rapid increase in bond failures at an early stage in the system's relaxation, shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrain}c. Across all strain values considered, we see a dramatic increase in bond failures at an early stage in the system's relaxation. The curves mirror that of the high temperature cases considered in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempBondResponse}. This is indicative of a large number of bonds being pushed close to their respective failure threshold and, as such, fail diffusely and independently. This level of diffuse damage can be observed in both cases shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots} where early bond failure occurs diffusely across the network and are unable to form macroscopic failures.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/HighGammaStrain/HighGammaStrainSnapshots.png}
    \caption[Snapshot of bond failures at low strains.]{Snapshot of bond failures after an imposed step strain of {\bf a)} $\gamma_0=0.9$ and {\bf b)} $\gamma_0=1.0$. The colour scale indicated the order in which bonds fail from green to blue.\newline
    Parameters: $E=0.35$, $x=0.0062$
    }
    \label{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}
\end{figure}

As observed in the low-strain regime, at high enough strains, networks still fail early during the relaxation, through a series of smaller macroscopic failures, rather than through a single macroscopic failure. Here, damage early on in the relaxation was enough to cause catastrophic failure within the network, with the system's strain quickly decreasing to $0$. This failure mechanism is illustrated in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}b. Notably, these failures are aligned along lines perpendicular to the principal strain axis. This alignment is discussed further in \cref{sec:DirectionOfFracture}.

Despite this large level of diffuse damage, which occurs early in the deformation, lower strain networks can persist for several more decades before they ultimately fail catastrophically in single brittle failure, illustrated in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}a. These networks exhibit little change in the overall stress response over this time, despite an increasing number of bond failures, as indicated in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrain}c. Here, the number of bond breakages before failure does not plateau with time, unlike what was observed at small strain in \cref{fig:LowGammaTempBondResponse} and \cref{fig:LowGammaStrainJoint}. The author suggests that this behaviour arises from stabilising fields that develop at high strain, which allow the network to remain relatively undeformed at the macroscopic level. As a result, the network can sustain a greater extent of bond failure than low-strain systems, while appearing largely unaffected.

While the number of bonds continues to increase with time $t$, the ultimate number of bonds at failure $B^*$ plateaus as $\gamma_0 \to 0.9$ to a value $B^*\sim 750$. We expect that these networks are like their low-strain counterparts and are waiting for a critical bond to fail that is in a configuration where large-scale crack propagation can occur. This is again characterised by the dramatic increase in the sample-to-sample variance as the failure time gets unpredictably delayed. 

This plateau in $B^*$, while $B$ continues to increase, may indicate a return to diffuse behaviour over a longer period of time, although this effect ultimately appears at a timescale longer than is computationally feasible. This effect may be minimised as a result of a better choice in the breakage threshold $E$, although the author was unable to find a better set of parameters that exemplifies this delayed failure phenomenon.

\section{Direction of fracture} \label{sec:DirectionOfFracture}

As discussed in \cref{sec:lowStrainResponse}, at small strain, the direction of crack propagation appears to be horizontal or vertical with roughly equal probability. Contrastingly, at high strains $\gamma_0\sim 1$, as shown in \cref{fig:HighGammaStrainSnapShots}, failure always propagates perpendicular to the principal strain axis, and approximately along a direction running from the top left to the bottom right of the sample.

Considering the purely affine deformation which occurs in the network, we can consider a bond which starts at an angle $\theta$ relative to the horizontal axis in the bond's rest configuration. Under a shear deformation of magnitude $\gamma_0$ in the $xy$ plane, the resultant affine deformation strains the bond such that its axial strain is
\begin{equation*}
    \epsilon_b(\theta| \gamma_0) = \sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)} - 1.
\end{equation*}
A more thorough discussion of this can be seen in \cref{sec:approxEx}. We plot in \cref{fig:AffineBondStrain}a, $\epsilon_b(\theta| \gamma_0)$ for the two cases of gamma we consider in this work, $\gamma_0=0.01$ and $\gamma_0=1$. 

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/CrackDirections/BondStrain.pdf}
    \caption[Illustrations of bond state under affine deformation]{
    Illustrations of {\bf a)} the resulting strain $\epsilon_b$ and {\bf b)} the angle $\theta^\prime$ resulting from an affine deformation of a bond initially at a principal angle $\theta$. We plot the two curves corresponding to the two strains considered in this work $\gamma_0=0.01$ and $\gamma_0=1$. The principal angle $\theta\in[0,\pi)$ is measured relative to the horizontal $x$-axis. \newline
    {\bf a)} The zero of each curve, indicated with a vertical line, shows the angle $\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi)$ at which the applied strain transitions from extension to compression.}
    \label{fig:AffineBondStrain}
\end{figure}

For small $\gamma_0$, the resultant affine strains remain small and close to zero with an equal split of bonds in both compression and extension. The magnitudes of the resultant strains also remain of similar size, although with a slight bias to elongated bonds, as is illustrated in the $\gamma_0=1$ case. Here, as the breakage condition considers the bond strain energy $E=\frac{\mu}{2}\epsilon_b^2$ rather than the bond strain $\epsilon_b$, the network's fracture is agnostic to the strain being compressive or extensional.   

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/CrackDirections/StrainDistributions.pdf}

    \label{fig:PDFBondDistributions}

As a result, the fracture propagates initially independent of the network structure, with the network appearing initially homogenous, as indicated by both \cref{fig:AffineBondStrain} and \cref{fig:PDFBondDistributions}a. The affine predictions shown in \cref{fig:AffineBondStrain} indicate little dependence on $\theta$ for both the resulting strain $\epsilon_b$ and angle $\theta^\prime$, which is reinforced by the distributions plotted in \cref{fig:PDFBondDistributions}a. Here, the affine deformation evenly distributes bonds with both positive and negative strain. As force balance is reimposed, this distribution progressively broadens, though importantly remains symmetric around $\epsilon_b=0$. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{chapters/UltraDelayedFracture/CrackDirections/CrackSnapshots.png}
    \caption[Snapshot of crack features plotted in the rest frame]{
    Snapshots of systems in the rest frame at failure. Arrows show the axes at $58^{\circ}$ and $148^{\circ}$ to the horizontal axis.
    {\bf a)} $\gamma_0=0.09$, $x=10^{-6}$, $E=5.025\times10^{-5}$. \newline
    {\bf b)} $\gamma_0=1.0$, $x=0.0062$, $E=0.35$. \newline
    The colour scale indicated the order in which bonds fail from green to blue.\newline
    }
    \label{fig:CrackDirections}
\end{figure}

Contrastingly, the affine distribution at $\gamma_0=1$ is asymmetric about $\epsilon_b=0$, with a clear bias towards positive (extensional) strains. As the network subsequently relaxes to restore force balance, this distribution broadens, producing a larger population of bonds with high strain magnitude. The resultant bonds, which are likely to fail, are therefore bonds whose initial angle in the rest frame $\theta\approx58^{\circ}$, the angle which produces the largest affine deformation. This crack failure mechanism is again illustrated in \cref{fig:CrackDirections}. We illustrate in black the line perpendicular to these maximally strained bonds, which shows a qualitative match to the failure observed in the network. The exact fracture direction will depend strongly on the exact network topology and the local level of non-affine arrangement.

Finally, of interest are the spider-like cracks which form in the small-$\gamma_0$ regime. While cracks at these small strains initially fail in both the horizontal and vertical directions, a defining feature of these single brittle events is the splitting at the crack tip. The primary fracture bifurcates into secondary branches that propagate along diagonal directions through the network. We expect this to be a result of two effects:
\needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item As the network fails, the network loses local rigidity, which allows for a large local strain to occur at the crack tip. This forces a change in direction as the network twists. 
    \item As bonds break and a crack forms, stress can no longer be transmitted across the failed region. This modifies the form of the local stress propagator from the typical Eshelby propagator, now promoting the failure of bonds off the vertical and horizontal axes. 
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, these observations suggest that the direction of fracture in these networks is controlled by a competition between the initially imposed affine strain field and the subsequent redistribution of stress during relaxation and failure. At small strains, the affine strain distribution and resulting stress field render the network effectively isotropic with respect to fracture. This allows for cracks to fail to nucleate and propagate along either horizontal or vertical directions with comparable likelihood, with subsequent branching arising from local rearrangements at the crack tip.

At high strain, however, the anisotropic strains resulting from the affine deformation strongly bias the population of highly strained bonds toward a well-defined orientation. After relaxation and force balance, these bonds select a preferred fracture direction perpendicular to these maximally loaded bonds. While non-affine rearrangements and network structure introduce variability into the precise failure path, the overall fracture direction is nevertheless governed by the underlying strain-induced anisotropy of the bond loading.

\section{Conclusion}
In this chapter, we have investigated the delayed failure of soft network materials under a simple set of assumptions. Our central aim has been to demonstrate that the phenomenon characterised by Lockwood \etal~\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025} can be reproduced using direct simulations of network materials. We have demonstrated, within a minimal thermal model inspired by the SGR model, that the failure time of the network can be delayed to extremely long times following the imposed deformation. In this regime, the networks can appear macroscopically stable, exhibiting little to no detectable precursors in the bulk properties before the eventual rupture of a critical bond, which ultimately triggers a catastrophic, brittle failure of the system.

At low imposed step strains, we found that networks that ultimately undergo ultra-delayed failure initially relax in a manner that is almost indistinguishable from an equivalent athermal network in which bonds are not permitted to break. Following this initial non-affine relaxation, the system enters a long-lived stable state in which further mechanical rearrangements are minimal. In this state, bond failure is rare and controlled by the thermal fluctuation in the bonds over slow deterministic mechanical processes. The rupture of bonds is therefore well described by a stochastic Poisson process, as each bond explores its local energy well and occasionally escapes over its energy barrier. 

The resulting macroscopic fractures in these networks typically nucleate from the failure of a single critical bond, which happens to reside in a local configuration that can trigger a cascade of correlated failures. Once initiated, this cascade of breakages leads to a brittle, system-spanning failure. In the low-strain regime, these cracks predominantly propagate along horizontal or vertical directions in the network’s rest frame, consistent with the near-isotropic distribution of bond strains. As the cracks form, we note the formation of secondary branching as the network's local structure changes, resulting in the fracture changing direction and splitting. We argued that this behaviour arises from a combination of local loss of rigidity at the crack tip and modifications to the stress propagator as the network becomes damaged.

In contrast, at large imposed strains, the network is driven far beyond its elastic regime, and substantial non-affine rearrangements occur during relaxation. Here, a large number of aligned bonds are pushed close to their failure thresholds immediately after the step strain, leading to widespread, spatially diffuse bond breakage at early times. Despite this early damage, we found that networks can still exhibit ultra-delayed failure, persisting for many decades in time before ultimately fracturing. Interestingly, in this regime, the material can sustain a much larger number of broken bonds before macroscopic failure compared to the low-strain case, suggesting that the strain stiffening exhibited by highly deformed networks allows the network to accommodate progressive damage without immediate collapse.

Taken together, these results indicate that, despite the simplicity of the model, we have captured the essential physical ingredients underlying ultra-delayed failure resulting from thermal effects. In these disordered network the interplay between non-affine rearrangements, thermally activated bond failures, and stress redistribution controls the network's response. The fact that qualitatively similar behaviour emerges across different parameter regimes and in pre-existing models indicates that delayed yielding is not a fragile artefact of the model. At the same time, the complex and often unpredictable 
nature of failure, from time to crack structure, raises new questions about the 
microscopic mechanisms that ultimately govern when and where catastrophic failure 
nucleates. We therefore suggest that future work in this area could include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\bf Alternative network models} -- In this work, we have considered networks in the thermal regime through the introduction of a rate of bond failure dependent on a noise temperature $x$. A common method of introducing temperature in molecular dynamics simulations is through the introduction of a Brownian or Langevin thermostat \cite{allen_computer_2017}. Here, instead, thermal fluctuations are introduced explicitly at the level of the node dynamics with the node positions being driven by a stochastic equation. Work done by Tauber \etal in Ref.~\cite{tauber_role_2020} showed that an increase in temperature corresponded to a more homogeneous stress redistribution and induced increased thermally activated failure of springs, equivalent to our failure rate condition defined in \cref{eq:SGRRate}. 

    We would expect that the results from these two models would show qualitatively similar results. In this formulation, however, the temperature $T$ is instead considered an explicit temperature rather than a noise parameter, as the bond energies by definition are considered on the same scale as temperature. This model may therefore be a better representation of elastomers and hydrogels, which are known to be governed by thermal fluctuations and entropy \cite{arruda_three-dimensional_1993,james_theory_1943}. These models prove to be significantly more computationally challenging, requiring a much smaller time-step than the overdamped equations used elsewhere \cite{leimkuhler_robust_2013}. 
    [Early experiments by the author into this model proved successful, although they were unable to reach the system sizes and timescales needed to characterise the dynamics.]

    \item {\bf Athermal fracture} -- As discussed in \cref{sec:delayedInto}, we have only considered delayed failure resulting from the thermally delayed failure in the material, considering athermal failure to be highly unlikely. In the highly connected regime ($z\approx 5.4 \gg z_c$), for which the networks considered in this work exist, relaxation times are short relative to the failure time of the bonds. As the network connectivity is tuned towards $z_c$, the relaxation times increase, diverging as a power law at $z_c$\cite{shivers_strain-controlled_2023}. Here, while the network's relaxation times increase, the sample-to-sample variance of systems' fracture strain also increases \cite{zhang_fiber_2017}. Therefore, while this route may lead to a mechanism for ultra-delayed failure tunable by the system's connectivity and imposed strain $\gamma_0$, the behaviour will be highly dependent on the network's specific structure. 
    
    \item {\bf Stress redistribution and Critical bonds} -- A key question raised by the results presented in this work concerns how stress is redistributed during fracture. Much of the existing literature on this problem considers the evolution of a crack tip under predominantly elongational loading perpendicular to the fracture plane \cite{sanner_less_2025, bolander_fracture_1998, shekhawat_damage_2013, slepyan_models_2002, ju_role_nodate}, a regime in which the local deformations near the crack tip are always large. In contrast, in the low-strain regime studied here, we here we observe an abrupt transition in fracture behaviour at some point during the failure. This suggests that the local stress redistribution evolves qualitatively during propagation. 

    Further analytical progress may be possible by extending classical treatments of the Eshelby stress propagator \cite{picard_elastic_2004, eshelby_determination_1997} to account for the presence of non-homogeneous fracture surfaces or more complex wall geometries, resembling those created by pre-existing damage. Such approaches could provide deeper insight into how stress is redistributed in the vicinity of evolving cracks in disordered networks.

    Finally, we introduced the concept of a critical bond, whose failure resulted in the immediate catastrophic failure of the network. The existence of such critical bonds or regions of heightened susceptibility has been widely observed in amorphous materials, both experimentally and computationally \cite{mattsson_time-dependent_2017,cipelletti_microscopic_2020,yu_shortest_2024,membrillo_solis_tracking_2022,ozawaRareEventsDisorder2022,aime_microscopic_2018,nabizadeh_network_2024}. We hope that continued advances in experimental techniques \cite{porr_probing_2025} and numerical or data-driven methods \cite{mangal_predicting_2024,mangal_topological_2023,cubuk_identifying_2015} will enable the identification of where such failures nucleate, and ultimately provide routes to predicting or controlling their dynamics.
    
    \item {\bf Relation to gel collapse in colloidal networks} -- A potentially related phenomenon occurs in colloidal gels and arrested particle networks, for which experiments have shown that these materials can support their own weight for long times before ultimately collapsing catastrophically \cite{harich_gravitational_2016,bartlett_sudden_2012,manley_gravitational_2005,starrs_collapse_2002}. It has been shown in these systems that the network never truly reaches a static state: individual bonds continuously break and reform due to thermal fluctuations, and the microstructure slowly evolves. Therefore it this form of delayed yielding has been interpreted as a form of thermally activated creep rupture, where rare microscopic rearrangements progressively weaken load-bearing strands until a critical failure event nucleates \cite{blindstrom_structures_2012,teece_gels_2014,sprakel_stress_2011}.
    
    These behaviours suggest a natural analogy with the ultra-delayed failures observed here under step strain: in both cases, the systems appear from a macroscopic perspective quasi-stationary over long times, while microscopic damage continues, and rare events at the bond or strand level nucleate the ultimate instability. A useful direction for future work is to consider the model described above, to compare the resulting statistics of delay times and precursor activity against experiments.

    Ultimately, this may be able to separate the purely dynamics-based delay routes from the solvent-mediated mechanisms, alternatively proposed as a source of delayed failure \cite{harich_gravitational_2016,varga_modelling_2018,padmanabhan_gravitational_2018}. Here, hydrodynamical erosion of the gel forms fluidised channels (\quotes{streamers}), which have been proposed to destabilise the gel's structure. Establishing which ingredients are essential across these systems would clarify whether ultra-delayed instabilities are a generic consequence of thermally activated rupture in disordered networks, or whether additional hydrodynamical couplings are required in the colloidal case.
    
    \item {\bf Experimental studies with the protocol of step shear strain} -- At present, there remains limited direct experimental evidence for ultra-delayed banding or failure under a true step strain protocol. Most work has been limited to short timescale failure in polymer melts \cite{boukany_exploring_2009,boukany_step_2009,barroso_evaluation_2002,einaga_stress_1973} or simple constitutive models \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_nonmonotonic_2009}. With consequences for material manufacturing industries, it is important to test this experimentally, in both network and more generally amorphous materials. The major problem with observing this experimentally is the parametrisation of the system such that the failure time ultimately occurs within experimental timescales, but also late enough to be defined as delayed rather than immediate failure. While a true step strain protocol is inaccessible experimentally, a \quotes{rapid strain ramp} at a rate quick enough to avoid any failure before the maximum strain $\gamma_0$ is reached is achievable~\cite{barroso_evaluation_2002}. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Calculating Approximate free parameters in Breakage Rate} \label{sec:approxEx}
Predicting the relaxed post-strain configurations of disordered networks is inherently challenging. This problem has been extensively studied in the context of rigidity (see \cref{sec:Rigidity}). Recent work exploring the high-dimensional energy landscapes of such networks has demonstrated that predicting their evolution is computationally intractable without direct numerical simulation \cite{damavandi_energetic_2022,damavandi_energetic_2022-1}. These difficulties arise primarily from the large non-affine rearrangements characteristic of semi-flexible networks. As the final parameter ranges will ultimately be refined through systematic parameter space searches, we require only a reasonable starting point rather than an exact prediction.


To obtain such an estimate, we first consider a single bond of rest length $l_0$ oriented at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal axis, with one node fixed at the origin. Under a simple shear deformation characterised by the matrix
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        1 & \gamma_0 \\
        0 & 1 \\
    \end{matrix}\right)
\end{equation*}
the initially free end of the bond, which starts at $(l_0\cos\theta,\, l_0\sin\theta)$, is transformed to
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        1 & \gamma_0 \\
        0 & 1 \\
    \end{matrix}\right)
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        l_0\cos(\theta) \\
        l_0\sin(\theta) \\
    \end{matrix}\right)
    =
    \left(\begin{matrix}
        l_0(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta)) \\
        l_0\sin(\theta) \\
    \end{matrix}\right).
\end{equation*}
The post-shear bond length is therefore
\begin{equation}
    l=l_0\sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding bond strain is
\begin{align}
     \epsilon_b(\theta| \gamma_0) &= \frac{l_0\sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)} -l_0}{l_0} \\
     &= \sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)} - 1.
\end{align}
This expression for $\epsilon_b(\theta)$ neglects any non-affine rearrangements of the surrounding network and should therefore be interpreted as an approximate upper bound on the expected bond strains.

For the large, disordered networks considered here, we assume that bond orientations are approximately uniformly distributed. We therefore consider the maximum value of $\epsilon_b(\theta|\gamma_0)$ over $\theta \in [0,\pi)$ and define
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\max}(\gamma_0) = \max_{\theta \in [0,\pi)} \left\{ \epsilon_b(\theta|\gamma_0) \right\}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding maximum expected bond energy at strain $\gamma_0$ is then $\mu \epsilon_{\max}^2/2$.

As the networks we consider are large, we will assume that bonds start in a roughly uniform distribution of angles. We therefore consider the maximum values of $\epsilon_b(\theta | \gamma_0)$ in the range $\theta \in [0,\pi)$. We will refer to this as $\epsilon_{max}(\gamma_0) = \max\left\{ \epsilon_b(\theta | \gamma_0) : \theta \in [0,\pi) \right\}$. The maximum expected strain energy for a given strain $\gamma_0$ is therefore $\mu\epsilon_{max}^2/2$. We then solve for this at the centre of the chosen strain range and introduce a safety factor of two to account for the neglected non-affine rearrangements, which provides a reasonable starting point.

The corresponding maximum expected bond energy at strain $\gamma_0$ is then $\mu \epsilon_{\max}^2/2$. We estimate a suitable central value for the strain parameters by solving for this energy at the centre of the parameter range of interest and introducing a safety factor of approximately two to account for the neglected non-affine effects. This provides a practical starting point for our parameter exploration.

We adopt a similar strategy to obtain an approximate starting value for the temperature-like parameter $x$. Here we consider the mean bond energy, defined as
\begin{equation*}
    \langle \epsilon_b \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\mu}{2} \epsilon_b(\theta | \gamma_0)^2 d\theta.
\end{equation*}
Substituting this average strain into the failure rate given in \cref{eq:SGRRate} yields an average bond failure rate $\langle \Gamma \rangle$ for a typical bond in the system. Assuming that bond failures follow a Poisson process, such that the mean lifetime equals $1/\langle \Gamma \rangle$, we choose $x$ such that the average bond survives for a time $\langle \Gamma \rangle \approx 10^{20}$ in simulation units.

These estimates provide a reasonable initial parameter set. However, additional tuning is required to identify parameter combinations that best capture the relevant physics across the full range of behaviours studied in the main text.

\section{Strain Distribution for an Affine Deformation} \label{sec:affineStrainDist}
As discussed in \cref{sec:approxEx}, the expected strain following an affine deformation of magnitude $\gamma_0$, for a bond with principal angle $\theta\in[0,\pi)$ is
\begin{equation}
         \epsilon_b(\theta| \gamma_0) = \sqrt{(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta)} - 1. \label{eq:affine1}
\end{equation}
We define $\lambda\coloneq\epsilon_b+1$ and squaring \cref{eq:affine1} we obtain
\begin{align*}
        \lambda^2 
        &=(\cos(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(\theta))^2 + \sin^2(\theta) \\
        &= 1+\gamma_0^2\sin^2(\theta) + \gamma_0\sin(2\theta) \\
        &= 1 + \gamma_0^2\left[\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos(2\theta)\right] + \gamma_0\sin(2\theta)\\
        &= 1+ \frac{\gamma_0^2}{2} - \frac{\gamma_0^2}{2}\cos(2\theta)+\gamma_0\sin(2\theta)
\end{align*}
By defining $C\coloneq1+ \frac{\gamma_0^2}{2}$, $q\coloneq- \frac{\gamma_0^2}{2}$ and using the identify $\gamma_0\sin(2\theta)+q\cos(2\theta)=R\sin(2\theta + \phi)$, where 
\begin{equation*}
    R=\sqrt{\gamma_0^2+q^2}=\sqrt{\gamma_0^2+\frac{\gamma_0^4}{4}},
\end{equation*}
we can express that
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda^2=C+R\sin(2\theta+\phi)
\end{equation*}
where $\phi$ solves $\tan(\phi) = -\gamma_0/2$.

If we assume that the initial bond angle takes the form of a uniform distribution defined by $\theta \sim \text{Unif}[0,\pi)$, then $\alpha\coloneq2\theta+\phi$ also takes the form of a uniform distribution on $[0,2\pi)$. The distribution $U$ of $\sin(\alpha)$ has a known form 
\[
    f(u)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-u^2}}, \qquad u\in[-1,1].
\]
We can consider the affine transformation of $U$ to $\lambda^2$, $\lambda^2 = C + R U$.
Inverting this relation gives
\[
    U = \frac{\lambda^2 - C}{R} = \frac{(\epsilon+1)^2 - C}{R}.
\]
A change of variables then yields the probability density function of $\epsilon$ as
\begin{align*}
    f_\epsilon(\epsilon)
    &= f\left(\frac{(\epsilon+1)^2-C}{R}\right)
      \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}
      \left(\frac{(\epsilon+1)^2-C}{R}\right)\right| \\
    &= \frac{2(\epsilon+1)}
      {\pi R\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{(\epsilon+1)^2-C}{R}\right)^2}},
\end{align*}
with bounds determined by $U\in[-1,1]$. This implies
\[
    \epsilon \in \left[\sqrt{C-R}-1,\; \sqrt{C+R}-1\right].
\]

When varying the absolute error tolerance $\tau$ in the adaptive time-stepping scheme defined in \cref{sec:NetworkDynamics}, we observe little quantitative change in the relaxation dynamics. As shown in \cref{fig:DelayedConvergence}a, the response remains essentially unchanged as $\tau$ is varied across four orders of magnitude, from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-5}$. We therefore adopt the smallest tolerance that still permits reasonable computation times, which we take to be $\tau = 10^{-4}$. The Bernoulli trials governing bond breaking, defined using the rate in \cref{eq:SGRRate}, remain numerically accurate across all time step sizes. Nevertheless, we impose an upper bound on the adaptive time step of $\delta t = 0.1$ to ensure that individual bond failure times are resolved reliably.

In \cref{fig:DelayedConvergence}b, we further demonstrate that the characteristic failure time $t^*$ converges with increasing system size. All results presented in this chapter were therefore obtained using networks containing $N = 64{,}000$ nodes.
