\chapter{Ultradelayed Material Failure via Shear Banding After Straining in Networks} \label{sec:Ultradelayed}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter will study the behaviour of network materials under the step strain protocol outlined in \cref{sec:DeformationProtocols}. In particular, we will be exploring the same physics explored by Lockwood \textit{et al.} in Ref.\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}. Using a variety of constitutive models, this work explored the formation of delayed dramatic shear banding instabilities long after the initial imposition of the strain. We hope that by introducing thermal effects to the simple network models described in \cref{sec:Networks}, we can find and explore how this effect is presented in a more realistic model. 

Shear banding and failure have been widely explored both experimentally and theoretically, as discussed in \cref{sec:ShearBanding}, and have shown to show failure in a range of protocols. Typically, protocols such as shear startup, outlined in \cref{sec:DeformationProtocols}, where at some time $t=0$ the system is started to be sheared with a rate $\dot{\gamma}$ have been used to study the formation of these bands \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,pollard_yielding_2022,moorcroft_age-dependent_2011,divouxTransientShearBanding2010,divoux_stress_2011,kurokawa_avalanche-like_2015}. Here shear stress $\Sigma_{xy}$ accumulates roughly linearly with the applied strain $\gamma = \dot{\gamma}t$, until it reaches a maximum value where yielding initiates and the stress decays to a flowing steady state. Alternatively, the material is often subjected to a fixed stress of amplitude $\Sigma$ for $t \geq 0$ (a stress-controlled regime). Here, the material relaxes slowly with the material's strain $\gamma$ creeping forward with a rate that decays as $\dot{\gamma} \sim t^{-\alpha}$, with $\alpha \in \left( 0,1 \right)$, before suddenly failing as the material yield \cite{miguel_dislocation_2002,gibaud_heterogeneous_2010,leocmach_creep_2014,ballesta_creep_2016,liuCreepDynamicsAthermal2018,cabriolu_precursors_2019,siebenburger_creep_2012}.

In both cases, damage in the material accumulates over time either by increasing strain triggering yielding events or through stress propagation originating from a previous yielding event. Although during the initial stages of deformation, the shear response of the material is often macroscopically uniform, as this damage accumulates, this homogeneous state typically breaks down, and deformation becomes spatially heterogeneous. This shear localisation eventually passes a critical point, and the flow reorganises into shear bands with contrasting strain rates, separated by interfaces aligned along the shear gradient direction. This effect has been shown theoretically within a minimal set of continuum rheological constitutive assumptions \cite{fielding_shear_2014,moorcroft_age-dependent_2011,moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_transient_2011,fielding_triggers_2016}

Instead, we consider a step strain protocol where at time $t=0$ we rapidly deform the material to a finite fixed shear strain $\gamma_0$, where this deformation is held constant thereafter. Unlike the previous two deformation where the strain is unbounded and as such can support a final fluidised state, it has been assumed that the relaxation process of these materials is trivial, with the material stress either decaying to a constant value, or catastrophically decreasing as the material fails, depending on the magnitude of the imposed strain $\gamma_0$. A schematic illustration of these two responses is plotted in \cref{fig:trivialRelaxation}. Instead, in the work done by Lockwood, it is shown that the material may suffer a dramatic internal shear
banding instability long after the imposed deformation\cite{lockwood_ultradelayed_2025}. Importantly, this delay time can be delayed arbitrarily with little to no change to the macroscopic observables in the material. Therefore, an observer who lacks any knowledge of the materials' history could be caught entirely unawares by the instability.

As the step strain protocol is less commonly studied compared to others, little work exists before the work by Lockwood \textit{et al.}. Some alternative theoretical studies looked at the formation of banding over short timescales as the material fails immediately \cite{moorcroft_shear_2014,moorcroft_criteria_2013,adams_nonmonotonic_2009}. And experimentally in polymer melts, the same short timescale failures have been observed \cite{boukany_exploring_2009,boukany_step_2009,barroso_evaluation_2002,einaga_stress_1973}. Experiments examining the longer timescale dynamics after the system appears to have reached equilibrium are even less explored, although some studies have shown signs of delayed yielding similar to what is presented in Ref. \cite {lockwood_ultradelayed_2025} and to the results we present later \cite{boukany_step_2009,jacob_rheological_2019,van_der_kooij_laser_2018}.

Contrastingly, the step strain protocol has been well explored in the context of unbreakable fibre networks by Shivers \textit{et al.} in Ref.\cite{shivers_strain-controlled_2023}. We replicate the relevant physics in \cref{fig:networkRelaxation}, although a more detailed investigation of the relaxation timescales and dynamics is considered in the paper, but is not relevant to the work done in this chapter. Important to this work is the strain stiffening point, which exists for networks with connectivities $z<4$. Below this point, the network is naturally floppy with many zero-energy modes. After a critical point $\gamma_c(z_c)$, these modes vanish, and the network can hold stress similar to that of the network above the isostatic point $z>4$. We consider the origins of this transition more thoroughly in \cref{sec:Rigidity}.

To explore the ultra-delayed failure of networks, we extend the network model outlined in \cref{sec:Networks}, specifically focusing on the Jamming-based networks defined in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}. We will introduce temperature through two methods, a Brownian simulation where node positions are randomly \quotes{kicked}, and a Boltzmann-like breakage rate for the network bonds inspired by the soft glassy rheology model\cite{sollich_rheology_1997,sollich_soft_2006}. 

The bulk of the results in this work will use the latter method, which we introduced for its computational simplicity and ability to reach long time scales. In both models, shear is applied through applying Lees-Edwards boundary conditions (outlined in \cref{sec:Lees-Edwards}). This imposes effective periodic boundary conditions in all directions and enforces a simple shear in the $xy$ plane. The jamming-based networks used in this chapter are generated as outlined in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}, with $\phi = 1.0$ and the box size $L_x=L_y = \sqrt{N}$ determining the length scale of the system. Unless otherwise states this gives networks with connectivities of $z \approx 5.4$. Further, we also take units where $\mu_m=1.0$. 

\section{Brownian Dynamics} \label{sec:UltraDelayedBrownian}
The simplest model, which introduces a temperature $T$ is Brownian dynamics. We amend the dynamics defined in \cref{sec:NetworkDynamics} to be of the following form
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\bm{r}_i} = \frac{1}{\zeta}\bm{F}_{n,i} + \bm{v}_f(\bm{r}_i) + \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{\zeta}}R(t). \label{eq:Brownian}
\end{equation}
We work in unit where drag coefficient $\zeta = 1$, Boltzmann constant $k_B=1$ and $R(t)$ is a Gaussian white noise term with $\langle R(t)\rangle = 0$ and $\langle R(t) R(t^\prime) \rangle = \delta(t-t^\prime)$. This is equivalent to the limiting cases of the dynamics explored in Ref.\cite{tauber_role_2020}. We integrate \cref{eq:Brownian} using a simple Euler algorithm with time-step $\delta t = 10^{-3}$. During the simulation, we will take bonds to break irreversibly when their strain $\epsilon_b = (l-l_0)/l_0$ exceeds a threshold $\lambda$, which is evaluated after each dynamics step.

\section{Boltzman rate Fracture} \label{sec:BoltzmanFracture}
To reduce the computation time of the model, we need an alternative method of thermal fracture which allows for an increased time step while remaining numerically stable. With this aim, we will consider an alternative fracture condition inspired by the Soft Glassy Rheology (SGR) model \cite{sollich_rheological_1998,sollich_thermodynamic_2012}.

where each bond sits in a local energy well, defined by a global breaking energy $E$, local elastic strain energy ${\mu}{(l-l_0)^2}/{2l_0^2}$ and an effective noise temperature $x$. Each bond then has the ability to hop out of its energy well and break at a rate $\Gamma$ given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:SGRRate}
    \Gamma = \Gamma_0 \min \left\{ \exp \left[ \frac{E - \frac{\mu}{2}\frac{(l-l_0)^2}{l_0^2}}{x} \right], 1 \right\}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_0$ is a microscopic breaking rate. We work in units of time where $\Gamma_0 = 1$ and the rate is given a maximum value of $1$ in order to ensure the model remains thermodynamically consistent \cite{sollich_thermodynamic_2012}.

For systems in which the thermal energy is of the same order as the depth of the energy well the temperature parameter can be interpreted as the true thermodynamic temperature, with $x = k_B T$ \cite{sollich_thermodynamic_2012}. However, in our network system, where the energy depths are much larger than the thermal contributions, the effective noise temperature is related to how stress would propagate across the microscopic structure of the bond.
