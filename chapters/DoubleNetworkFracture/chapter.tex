\section{Methodology}
To capture the salient physics of double network materials, we must extend the network model defined in \cref{sec:NetworkStructure} and used in the previous chapters to model both a sacrificial network and a matrix network and how they interact with each other. As a starting point for our model, we again consider a worm-like chain model with Hamiltonian \cref{eq:NetworkHamiltonian}, again only considering the central forces acting on the nodes. We will take each network to evolve almost independently under \cref{eq:NetworkHamiltonian} as in the single network case, again ignoring the volume exclusion principle, both within each network, but also in interactions between the two networks. To connect the two networks, we will consider a subset of nodes in each network to be shared, allowing for the two networks to interact via these inter-network connections. These inter-network connections may correspond to either chemically bonded interactions or physical entanglement points between the two networks, and in either case, they enable load sharing, which has been observed experimentally \cite{ahmedBrittleDuctileTransition2014}. Each bond in our system can then be given a modulus $\mu_s$ and $\mu_m$, and a breakage threshold $\lambda_s$ and $\lambda_m$ for the sacrificial and matrix networks, respectively. We will consider a bond to break immediately when its own strain $l/l_0 - 1$ exceeds its corresponding threshold: $\lambda_s$ for sacrificial bonds or $\lambda_m$ for matrix bonds.

In the following work, we focus on Jammed Packing derived networks (described in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}); square lattice networks (described in \cref{sec:LatticeBasedNetworks}) will be discussed in \cref{sec:SquareAppendix}. These two constructions provide distinct approaches for generating double network geometries, by defining how each constituent network is constructed relative to the other and how the subset of nodes shared between the two networks is defined. Crucially, both methods define a relative length scale difference $M$ between the two networks (sacrificial and matrix), which will allow us to capture both the relevant microphysics as well as the macroscopic physics of bulk failure. 

\subsection{Jamming-based Double Networks}
For Jamming-based double networks, we generate the sacrificial network as in the single network case described in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks} by minimising an ensemble of $N_s$ disks with bidispersed radii $R_s$ and $1.4R_s$, each in equal numbers at a packing fraction of $\phi = 1.0$. We set the typical length scale of a sacrificial bond by defining the size of our periodic unit cell to be $L_{x0} = L_{y0} = \sqrt{N_s}$ as previously done. Overlapping pairs of nodes are then connected by a bond with its natural length $l_0$ being set to the distance between the centres of the two disks. 

The matrix network is constructed similarly, but now starting with an ensemble of $N_m$ disks $R_s$ and $1.4R_s$ in equal numbers at a packing fraction of $\phi = 1.0$, where $R_m \leq R_s$. The ratio of $R_s$ to $R_m$ defines $M = R_s / R_m \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, which now takes any positive real value, since by construction the networks do not need to be commensurate to define the internetwork connections. To define the internetwork connections between the sacrificial and matrix networks, we take the existing nodes used to generate the sacrificial network, scale them by a factor of $1/M$ and pin them to their corresponding nodes in the sacrificial network, with the remaining $N_m - N_s$ disks randomly initialised. 

The matrix ensemble is then equilibrated, taking care to ensure a homogeneous distribution of disks despite the inclusions introduced by the pinioned sacrificial disks, with matrix bonds formed in the same manner as in the sacrificial network. The double network can be formed by overlaying the unit cells of the two networks and introducing inter-network connections at all shared nodes, which correspond to all sacrificial nodes by construction.

\section{Rheological Protocol} \label{sec:NetworkQuasistatic}
Unlike the previous chapter, where we considered systems deformed under simple shear, we consider the system to be subjected to pure shear, with the principal extension axis aligned with the $y$-direction (vertical axis). We deform the square unit cell with initial dimensions $L_{x,0} = L_{x,0}$ along the y-axis and periodic boundary conditions in both axes, preserving the area $L_0^2$ of the unit cell through an effective compression in the $x$-direction. The deformation in the system will be measured by the global engineering strain in the system along the $y$-axis $\epsilon = L_{y}/L_{y,0} - 1$, with the strain rate $\dot{\epsilon}$ considered to be quasi-static, that is $\dot{\epsilon} \to 0$ and the system to be a constant thermodynamic equilibrium.

Numerically, this is done by considering steps in the deformation of size $\Delta\epsilon$, which by default are of size $\Delta\epsilon = 10^{-3}$, and for the system to equilibrate with a root mean square (r.m.s.) force on the nodes of magnitude $< 10^{-6}$ (We feel this is small enough to minimised the system size effects seen in \cite{dussi_athermal_2020}.) When no breakages occur, and $\Delta\epsilon$ is small enough, this process approximates the system's true quasi-static path. 

However, taking a step of size $\Delta\epsilon$ may be large enough to overstep the precise strain $\epsilon$ at which a singular bond fails, obscuring the true ordering of failures and thereby modifying the resulting fracture of the system from the true quasi-static response. To avoid this, we treat each deformation step as a \textit{trial} step consisting of an affine deformation of size $\Delta\epsilon$, followed by relaxing the system while suppressing any bond breakages. If the relaxed configuration contains no bonds with strains greater than their threshold, the \textit{trial} step is accepted, and the process continues.

If, instead, the relaxed configuration contains bonds that exceed their failure thresholds, the \textit{trial} identifies that the step was too large and a smaller step needs to be considered. To do this, we define a function $f(x)$ as  
\begin{equation*}
    f(\Delta\epsilon) = \max_{i\in B} \left[ \frac{l_i(\Delta\epsilon)-l_{i,0}}{l_{i,0}} - \lambda_i \right],
\end{equation*}
where $B$ denotes the set of all remaining bonds, $l_i(\Delta\epsilon)$ is the relaxed length of bond $i$ after a trial step of size $\Delta\epsilon$, $l_{i,0}$ its natural length, and $\lambda_i$ its failure threshold. This tracks the bond which is closest to failure to failure. The sign of $f(\Delta\epsilon)$ therefore determines whether a step is acceptable:
\begin{align*}
    f(\Delta\epsilon) < 0 &\iff \text{no bond exceeds its threshold (\textit{trial} step is too small)}, \\
    f(\Delta\epsilon) = 0 &\iff \text{at least one bond is exactly at threshold (Critical \textit{trial} step size)}, \\
    f(\Delta\epsilon) > 0 &\iff \text{at least one bond exceeds its threshold (\textit{trial} step is too large)},
\end{align*}
Thus, identifying the correct step size reduces to finding the smallest $\Delta\epsilon >0$ such that $f(\Delta\epsilon) = 0$, which can be achieved by bisection on the range $(0,\Delta\epsilon_{Max}]$ where $\Delta\epsilon_{Max}=10^{-3}$ is the default \textit{trial} step size.

Once a suitable step size is found, the dynamics of the fracture are resolved under an overdamped dynamics described in \cref{sec:NetworkDynamics} where $\bm{v}_f=\bm{0}$ in our quasi-static regime while resolving the fracture. The resultant equation of motion of a node $i$ is therefore 
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\bm{r}}_i = \sum_{j}\mu_{ij} \frac{l_{ij}-l_{ij,0}}{l_{ij,0}}\frac{\bm{r}_{ij}}{\left\lVert \bm{r}_{ij} \right\rVert}, \text{ for all neighbours $j$ of $i$.}
    \label{eq:DoubleMotion}
\end{equation}
The spring modulus $\mu_{ij}$ takes either $\mu_s$ or $\mu_m$, and time units are set by the drag coefficient $\zeta=1$. We integrate \cref{eq:DoubleMotion} with an adaptive Euler-Heun algorithm (described in \cref{sec:EulerHeun}) with an absolute error tolerance of $10^{-2}$. Bond breakages are evaluated at the start of each time step, and the time step $\delta t$ is reset to $10^{-4}$ to account for the discontinuity resulting from the removed bond. The system dynamics evolves in this manner until the root-mean-square (r.m.s.) force at the nodes again falls below $10^{-6}$, where another trial step of size $\Delta\epsilon=10^{-3}$ is attempted.

In all cases, we choose units of stress such that $\mu_s = 1.0$ and the characteristic length of our sacrificial bonds is set by our network structure, but in both cases considered, it is such that the size of our unit cell $L_{x,0} = L_{x,0} = \sqrt{N_s}$. The length scale of our matrix bonds is then set by $M$, the typical number of matrix-bond lengths per sacrificial-bond length. For notational convenience, we will define $M=0$ to denote a system with only a sacrificial network. In line with the experimental literature discussed previously, we will consider cases where the sacrificial network is comparatively sparse ($M>1.0$), stiffer than the matrix ($\mu_m < 1.0$), and more brittle ($\lambda_m > 0.5$). The data we present subsequently is obtained by averaging over $5$ samples, and for each quantity, we report the mean and standard deviation. 

\section{Stress-Strain Response}

This work aims to explore how the simple mesoscopic model described above can be used to explore how the effect of differing length scales affects the fracture and behaviour of double networks, particularly focusing on how stress is redistributed across the networks following a plastic event, as is explored in \cite{tauber_microscopic_2020,tauber_microscopic_2020}. We will discuss the results of this model under the above-discussed Jamming-based and Square Lattice-Based double network topologies, with a focus on the Jamming-based double network. The fundamental results of this work are shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}, where we compare the stress-strain response of a double network material with its sacrificial and matrix components individually for both a Jamming-based double network and a square lattice-based double network respectively. In both cases, we see that the double network retains most of the toughness of the matrix network while still retaining the stiffness of the sacrificial network, particularly during the early elastic regime of deformation, compared to the components individually.

The stress-strain response of the single matrix network (blue curve in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}a) and single sacrificial network (red curve in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}a) shows a non-linear elastic stretching response, often found in the network materials\cite{feng_nonlinear_2016,licup_nonlinear_2016,storm_nonlinear_2005}, followed by abrupt brittle or quasi-brittle failure \cite{dussi_athermal_2020, tauber_stretchy_2022}. The double network (solid black curve in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}b) response mirror those of its components (red and blue curves for the sacrificial and matrix components respectively), with an initial non-linear elastic response, again commonly found in elastomers\cite{millereauMechanicsElastomericMolecular2018, ducrot_toughening_2014}, hydrogels\cite{gong_why_2010} and macroscopic composites\cite{kingMacroscaleDoubleNetworks2019, takahashiCreatingStiffTough2018} up until the first sacrificial bond break at a strain close to where the single sacrificial network fails. The double network, then, undergoes an irreversible softening response as the sacrificial bonds fail diffusely and its contribution to the response plateaus, which can be seen in both our model and experiments \cite{vernereyStatisticalDamageMechanics2018,millereauMechanicsElastomericMolecular2018}. The double network then ultimately goes on to break catastrophically through the same macroscopic failure observed in the single network components.

For each double network, we characterise its mechanical response by extracting its failure strain $\epsilon^*_d$, its failure stress $\Sigma^*_d$ (defined in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}) and initial modulus $G_s$, $G_m$ and $G_d$ of the sacrificial component, matrix component and combined double network, respectively (defined via the initial stress-strain slopes in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}). For a single network, we quantify the response in the same way, denoted then with the relevant subscript. However, in this case, only one modulus, either $G_s$ or $G_m$, is obtained, depending on which network is considered.

As discussed previously, a well-designed tough double network has two key properties:
\begin{enumerate}[I]
    \renewcommand{\theenumi}{\Roman{enumi}}
    \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\theenumi)}
    \item It inherits the toughness of soft and ductile matrix components, with a failure strain $\epsilon^*_D \approx \epsilon^*_m \gg \epsilon^*_s$.
    \item It inherits the stiffness of its stiff but brittle sacrificial component, with modulus $G_d \approx G_s \gg G_m$.
\end{enumerate}

We illustrate property I) in \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}a-b and \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagramsFail} and where we show how the failure strain $\epsilon^*$ changes as a function of our control parameters $M$, $\mu_m$ and $\lambda_m$. In \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagramsFail} we plot a phase space diagram of $M$ against $\mu_m$ for $\lambda_m=3.0$ showing an increased failure toughness as either increasing $M$ or $\mu_m$. This trend is shown more clearly in the upper solid line of \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}a which represents a horizontal slice of \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagramsFail} at $\mu_m=0.2$. For all values of $\lambda_m$ in \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}a we observe a constant pattern: as $M \to \infty$ the network's toughness transitions from being close to the sacrificial failure point $\epsilon^*_d \approx \epsilon^*_s$ towards the toughness of the matrix, with $\epsilon_d^* \to \epsilon_m^*$. We do note a less pronounced upwards trend for $\lambda_m=1.0$ where the matrix and sacrificial bonds have similar breakage thresholds; in this case, early failures in the sacrificial networks propagate enough stress to result in premature failures in the matrix. In contrast, the curves corresponding to $\lambda_m=2.0$ or $3.0$ show that allowing enough separation in the failure of the two bond types ensures that the failure requires a significant weakening in the sacrificial network before it fails. We will return to this behaviour in more detail later on when we discuss the role of stress propagation.

Property II) is considered in \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagramsMod} we observe the matrix contribution to the overall stiffness increasing (shown as a decrease in $G_s/G_d$) with $M$ and $\mu_m$ as would be expected. This again shows the trivial limit: the double network effectively just becomes a single matrix network, inheriting its softness and achieving II). We see this trend clearly \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}e-d where the decrease is more apparent. We do not plot the values relative to $\lambda_m$ since the reported moduli are taken are the initial slope of the stress-strain response, where no bonds have failed. As a result is independent of $\lambda_m$, $\lambda_s$, and we are unable to report the single network moduli in this context. We plot the same data in the inserts of \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}e-d, now using log–log axes. We rescale the data in both cases by the factor $1+M\mu_m$ (applied with respect to $M$ in panel (d) and $\mu_m$ in panel (e), revealing an underlying power-law $G_s/G_d \sim A\left( 1+M\mu_m \right)^\alpha$. Fitting $A$ and $\alpha$ to the data in \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagramsMod} gives a prefactor of $A\approx1.02$ and an exponent $\alpha \approx -0.862$.

Considering both these trends shown in figures \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagrams} and \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract} and how they relate to the desired double network properties, I) and II), we see that achieving the desired optimal double network properties requires a trade off to be made. Optimising property I), for a given $\lambda_m$, would lead to increasing $M$ and $\mu_m$ until the double network material fails like the matrix single matrix network materials (whose failure point $\epsilon^*_m$ only depends on $\lambda_m$). At this point, the sacrificial network contributes very little, and the single matrix network should be used due to its easier synthesis. On the other hand, decreasing $M$ or $\mu_m$ would lead to the sacrificial network dominating the stress-strain response; therefore, a compromise of the two parameters is required where we have a large enough $M$ for the failure strain to be large but a small enough $\mu_m$ for the sacrificial network to dominate $G_d$. In practice, we found that the region of parameter space exemplified by the values $M = 3.0$, $\mu_m = 0.2$, $\lambda_m = 3.0$ shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain} to be a sensible compromise.

\section{Damage in the Sacrificial Network}

To explore these results further, we will look at how the double networks fail as we move through parameter space and their behaviour compared to that of the corresponding single networks. We begin again with the specific set of networks shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}. In \cref{fig:JammedBrokenStrain}, the fraction of bonds broken $B^*$ is shown for the single matrix and single sacrificial networks in panel (a), and for the double network and its individual components in panel (b). The single matrix (solid blue line) shows a simple, highly brittle response with a dramatic increase in bond failures at the failure strain $\epsilon_m^d$. We see a similar but slightly more ductile response in the sacrificial network (solid red line) due to the $\lambda_s < \lambda_m$ (which matches the results seen in \cite{dussi_athermal_2020}), where we see a small number of failures prior to the network's ultimate brittle failure, shown in \cref{fig:JammedSacrifical}.

The double network eventually fails in the same way as the matrix network, via an abrupt and brittle failure of the matrix network. We note that most of the sacrificial network remains intact and continues to percolate vertically. Consequently, it still contributes, with diminishing contributions, to the double network materials, leading to a material which still performs better than the single matrix network.

The dependence on $M$ shows in \cref{fig:JammedBrokenExtract}a for the double network system is a consequence of the construction of our networks: as $M$ increases, the fraction of sacrificial bonds relative to the total number of bonds decreases. This dilution causes the total fraction of bonds $ B^* \to 0.0$ as $M\to\infty$. The non-monotonic behaviour at $M=2$, however, is a consequence of the sacrificial component itself. As shown in \cref{fig:JammedBrokenExtract}b, the fraction of broken sacrificial bonds at failure increases, showing an increase in the amount of diffuse microcracks in the sacrificial network.

Panels \cref{fig:JammedBrokenExtract}b and d show the corresponding dependence on $\mu_m$. In panel b, the total fraction of broken bonds in the double network increases monotonically, for $\lambda_m = 2.0, \; 3.0$. This again shows an implied trend of stress de-localisation, where we see an increase in the allowed sacrificial failure before the matrix ruptures. For $\lambda_m =1.0$ we see a small decrease in the $B^*$ before the expected monotonic increase. This does correspond with an increase in the standard deviation of our results over samples and may correspond to slight under-convergence in our athermal quasi-static algorithm (convergence was checked for major reported quantities $\epsilon^*$, $\Sigma^*$, and moduli).

In each plot, we show results for $\lambda_m=1.0$ , $2.0$, and $3,0$, which converge to different values of $B^*$ under both increasing $M \to \infty$ and $\mu_m \to 1$, this is consistent with the work done by \textit{Tauber et al} in a simple multi-spring model with $M=1$ \cite{tauber_microscopic_2020}. While a theoretical extension of this model to include $M > 1$ would provide a useful analytical framework for interpreting these trends, constructing such a model lies beyond the scope of the present thesis.

\subsection{Lattice Based Double Networks}
The simplest form of a single network structure is that of a lattice-based network. The sacrificial network is constructed as described in \cref{sec:LatticeBasedNetworks} by defining a set of lattice vectors and rules for connecting them. The matrix network can also be generated in the same way, however, with a scaled set of lattice vectors. For simplicity, we choose $M\in\mathbb{Z}$, such that the sacrificial and matrix lattices are commensurate: the matrix lattice vectors are integer multiples of the sacrificial ones. This ensures that the nodes of the matrix lattice lie exactly on a subset of the nodes of the fine sacrificial lattice. The natural length of each bond $l_0$ is then set in this configuration.  

While we choose to ignore the volume exclusion principle during the dynamics of the simulation to simplify the forces in our system, only to consider central forces, we can optionally decide to respect this in the network generation steps. This gives us the choice of whether we allow both a matrix and a sacrificial bond to perfectly lie on top of each other in the networks during the network's natural configuration. It's also possible to add further to subdivide the sacrificial bonds such that they connect at every lattice point of the matrix they pass over. While this would normally give four combinations of lattice network geometries, the case where we do not allow overlaps and do not subdivide the sacrificial bonds decouples the matrix and sacrificial networks, and therefore cannot be considered a double network. An example of the three remaining cases can be seen in \cref{fig:DoubleSquareTypes} for a square lattice with $M=4$.

\begin{figure}[hbt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{chapters/DoubleNetworkFracture/NetworkGeneration/SquareTypes-4.pdf}
    \caption[Figure detailing the types of network constructions for a square lattice double network with $M=4$.]{Figure detailing the types of network constructions for a square lattice double network with $M=4$. Each type differs in the choice of nodes which can interact with both the sacrificial and matrix networks. In the overlapping network, we ignore the volume exclusion principle and allow both a matrix and a sacrificial bond to co-exist. Networks with Subdivisions subdivide their sacrificial bonds into smaller sections to connect to the matrix nodes they pass over.}
    \label{fig:DoubleSquareTypes}
\end{figure}

In this chapter, we will consider square-lattice-based double networks whose intra-network connections have connectivity $z=z_c=4.0$. These networks sit on the isostatic point $z_c$ and are therefore only rigid in their initial state; any bond failures which occur during deformation will consequently locally become floppy. As discussed in \cref{sec:Rigidity}, the network may strain-stiffen during its deformation and behave as if it has a $z > z_c$ even after bond failures. To ensure that the network remains in the strain-stiffened regime throughout its entire deformation, we pre-stress each bond by reducing its natural length $l_0$ such that its initial local bond strain $\epsilon_{b}$ is sampled from a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\epsilon_p, \sigma_p)$ with mean $\epsilon_p$ and width $\sigma_p$. By varying each bond's local strain, we address the problems described in \cref{sec:LatticeBasedNetworks}, usually solved by pruning and perturbing the natural positions of each network's nodes.
