\chapter{Toughness of Double Network Hydrogels} \label{sec:DoubleNetworks}

\section{Introduction}

Soft network materials, such as rubbers or gels, have the remarkable property of being able to undergo large deformations without losing elasticity.  This has led them to be used in numerous applications, including biomedical tissue engineering \cite{leeHydrogelsTissueEngineering2001}, stretchable electronics \cite{rogers_materials_2010}, and soft robotics \cite{martinezSoftActuatorsRobots2014}. However, many of these materials typically exhibit abrupt and brittle failures at low strains, which leads to inferior mechanical performance \cite{noauthor_50th_nodate}. One of the most promising strategies for overcoming this limitation is to introduce a second polymer network to the material, to create a so-called double network. These composite materials have proved to provide remarkable material properties in double network hydrogels \cite{gong_why_2010, nakajima_synthesis_2013, sun_highly_2012, xin_effect_2013}, elastomers \cite{ducrot_toughening_2014, millereauMechanicsElastomericMolecular2018} and macroscopic composites\cite{king_macroscale_2019,takahashi_creating_2018}.

The work done in this paper can be considered to focus on double network hydrogels, but we expect most results can be applied more generally to composite materials constructed of two network types. The pioneering work which has inspired the work in this chapter was done by Gong \textit{et al.} \cite{gong_double-network_2003}, who combined a stiff and weak \quotes{sacrificial} network with a soft and extensible \quotes{matrix} network. The resultant network retained the stiffness of the sacrificial component but obtained the failure toughness of the matrix component. 

Experimentally, double network hydrogels have been studied extensively, and, as such, there is already a wealth of insights into the failure mechanics of double network hydrogels. Small-angle neutron scattering probing the characteristic length scales of internal non-affine rearrangements has suggested that stress becomes delocalised during fracture \cite{ducrot_structure_2015, tominaga_molecular_2007}. Birefringence imaging further reveals that non-optimised double-double network structures exhibit large stress concentration, compared to more optimised structures, which distribute the stress more uniformly \cite{gong_double-network_2003, zheng_how_2021}. Dynamic light scattering experiments have demonstrated a strong coupling between fluctuations in the two networks, consistent with mutual entanglements and the ability for the two network components to share load \cite{huang_importance_2007}. Additionally, increasing the molar fraction of the matrix network, relative to the sacrificial network, favours the formation of diffuse microcracks, indicative of ductile behaviour, rather than the propagation of a single macroscopic crack associated with brittle failure \cite{fukao_effect_2020, ju_role_nodate}.

Early work in continuum models done by Tanaka\cite{tanaka_local_2007} and Brown\cite{brown_model_2007} looked at the breakage of the sacrificial bonds, which were stabilised by a background matrix network. These models assumed that the stress field in the material can be described by continuum mechanics, with both networks acting like a homogeneous elastic solid. These models were later extended to consider the evolution of damage in the material and have been able to describe the hysteresis in the stress-strain response, the progressive softening and the Mullins effect observed in double network hydrogels \cite{vernereyStatisticalDamageMechanics2018, wang_pseudo-elasticity_2011,bacca_model_2017,lavoie_continuum_2019,okumura_toughness_2004,zhao_theory_2012}. While these models can capture the macroscopic behaviours of the materials, they cannot capture the detailed underlying microphysics.

This microphysics can be captured using molecular dynamics simulations \cite{jang_mechanical_2007,wang_simulational_2017,higuchi_fracture_2018,mugnaiInterspeciesInteractionsDual2025,tauber_sharing_2021}. However, these models often fail to capture the necessary system sizes or explore enough of the required parameter space due to the high computational costs associated with these techniques. Therefore, mesoscale models, such as the one we will present later, try and capture a simplified version of the molecular dynamics simulation while still accessing large enough systems or parameter ranges have proven successful. 

Previous mesoscopic models, which we use as inspiration for our work, have looked at fracturing in single-component materials \cite{dussi_athermal_2020, lei_mesoscopic_2021}. Alternative double network frameworks have been proposed; however, they either fail to resolve the length scale difference \cite{tauber_microscopic_2020}, consider a sparse unbreakable network \cite{noguchi_fracture_2024}, or focus on the edge/necking effects of non-periodic systems \cite{li_fracture_2024}. 

This work aims to expand on these pre-existing models and explore how a simple mesoscopic model can be used to explore how the effect of differing length scales affects the fracture and behaviour of double networks, particularly focusing on how stress is redistributed across the networks following a plastic event. By explicitly considering the double network structure in a controlled manner, we aim to capture the relevant macro and micro physics while including minimal ingredients. 

\section{Methodology}
To capture the salient physics of double network materials, we must extend the network model defined in \cref{sec:NetworkStructure} and used in the previous chapters to model both a sacrificial network and a matrix network and how they interact with each other. As a starting point for our model, we again consider a worm-like chain model with the Hamiltonian defined in \cref{eq:NetworkHamiltonian}, again only considering the central forces acting on the nodes. We will take each node to evolve almost independently under \cref{eq:NetworkHamiltonian} as in the single network case, ignoring the volume exclusion principle, both within each network, but also in interactions between the two networks. To connect the two networks, we will consider a subset of nodes in each network to be shared, allowing for the two networks to interact via these inter-network connections. These inter-network connections may correspond to either chemically bonded interactions or physical entanglement points between the two networks, and in either case, they enable load sharing, which has been observed experimentally \cite{ahmedBrittleDuctileTransition2014}. Each bond in our system can then be given a modulus $\mu_s$ and $\mu_m$, and a breakage threshold $\lambda_s$ and $\lambda_m$ for the sacrificial and matrix networks, respectively. We will consider a bond to break immediately when its own strain $l/l_0 - 1$ exceeds its corresponding threshold: $\lambda_s$ for sacrificial bonds or $\lambda_m$ for matrix bonds.

In the following work, we focus on Jammed Packing derived networks (described in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks}); square lattice-based networks (described in \cref{sec:LatticeBasedNetworks}) will be discussed in \cref{sec:SquareAppendix}. These two constructions provide distinct approaches for generating double network geometries, by defining how each constituent network is constructed relative to the other and how the subset of nodes shared between the two networks is defined. Crucially, both methods define a relative length scale difference $M$ between the two networks (sacrificial and matrix), which will allow us to capture both the relevant microphysics as well as the macroscopic physics of bulk failure. 

\subsection{Jamming-based Double Networks}
For Jamming-based double networks, we generate the sacrificial network as in the single network case described in \cref{sec:PackingBasedNetworks} by minimising an ensemble of $N_s$ disks with bidispersed radii $R_s$ and $1.4R_s$, each in equal numbers at a packing fraction of $\phi = 1.0$. We set the typical length scale of a sacrificial bond by defining the size of our periodic unit cell to be $L_{x0} = L_{y0} = \sqrt{N_s}$ as previously done. Overlapping pairs of nodes are then connected by a bond with its natural length $l_0$ being set to the distance between the centres of the two disks. 

The matrix network is constructed similarly, but now starting with an ensemble of $N_m$ disks $R_s$ and $1.4R_s$ in equal numbers at a packing fraction of $\phi = 1.0$, where $R_m \leq R_s$. The ratio of $R_s$ to $R_m$ defines $M = R_s / R_m \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, which now takes any positive real value, since by construction the networks do not need to be commensurate to define the internetwork connections. To define the internetwork connections between the sacrificial and matrix networks, we take the existing nodes used to generate the sacrificial network, scale them by a factor of $1/M$ and pin them to their corresponding nodes in the sacrificial network, with the remaining $N_m - N_s$ disks randomly initialised. 

The matrix ensemble is then equilibrated, taking care to ensure a homogeneous distribution of disks despite the inclusions introduced by the pinioned sacrificial disks, with matrix bonds formed in the same manner as in the sacrificial network. The double network can be formed by overlaying the unit cells of the two networks and introducing inter-network connections at all shared nodes, which correspond to all sacrificial nodes by construction.

\section{Rheological Protocol} \label{sec:NetworkQuasistatic}
Unlike the previous chapter, where we considered systems deformed under simple shear, we consider the system to be subjected to pure shear, with the principal extension axis aligned with the $y$-direction (vertical axis). We deform the square unit cell with initial dimensions $L_{x,0} = L_{x,0}$ along the y-axis and periodic boundary conditions in both axes, preserving the area $L_0^2$ of the unit cell through an effective compression in the $x$-direction. The deformation in the system will be measured by the global engineering strain in the system along the $y$-axis $\epsilon = L_{y}/L_{y,0} - 1$, with the strain rate $\dot{\epsilon}$ considered to be quasi-static, that is $\dot{\epsilon} \to 0$ and the system to be a constant thermodynamic equilibrium.

Numerically, this is done by considering steps in the deformation of size $\Delta\epsilon$, which by default are of size $\Delta\epsilon = 10^{-3}$, and for the system to equilibrate with a root mean square (r.m.s.) force on the nodes of magnitude $< 10^{-6}$ (We feel this is small enough to minimised the system size effects seen in \cite{dussi_athermal_2020}.) When no breakages occur, and $\Delta\epsilon$ is small enough, this process approximates the system's true quasi-static path. 

However, taking a step of size $\Delta\epsilon$ may be large enough to overstep the precise strain $\epsilon$ at which a singular bond fails, obscuring the true ordering of failures and thereby modifying the resulting fracture of the system from the true quasi-static response. To avoid this, we treat each deformation step as a \textit{trial} step consisting of an affine deformation of size $\Delta\epsilon$, followed by relaxing the system while suppressing any bond breakages. If the relaxed configuration contains no bonds with strains greater than their threshold, the \textit{trial} step is accepted, and the process continues.

If, instead, the relaxed configuration contains bonds that exceed their failure thresholds, the \textit{trial} identifies that the step was too large and a smaller step needs to be considered. To do this, we define a function
\begin{equation*}
    f(\Delta\epsilon) = \max_{i\in B} \left[ \frac{l_i(\Delta\epsilon)-l_{i,0}}{l_{i,0}} - \lambda_i \right],
\end{equation*}
where $B$ denotes the set of all remaining bonds, $l_i(\Delta\epsilon)$ is the relaxed length of bond $i$ after a trial step of size $\Delta\epsilon$, $l_{i,0}$ its natural length, and $\lambda_i$ its failure threshold. This tracks the bond which is closest to failure to failure. The sign of $f(\Delta\epsilon)$ therefore determines whether a step is acceptable:
\begin{align*}
    f(\Delta\epsilon) < 0 &\iff \text{no bond exceeds its threshold (\textit{trial} step is too small)}, \\
    f(\Delta\epsilon) = 0 &\iff \text{at least one bond is exactly at threshold (Critical \textit{trial} step size)}, \\
    f(\Delta\epsilon) > 0 &\iff \text{at least one bond exceeds its threshold (\textit{trial} step is too large)},
\end{align*}
Thus, identifying the correct step size reduces to finding the smallest $\Delta\epsilon >0$ such that $f(\Delta\epsilon) = 0$, which can be achieved by bisection on the range $(0,\Delta\epsilon_{Max}]$ where $\Delta\epsilon_{Max}=10^{-3}$ is the default \textit{trial} step size.

Once a suitable step size is found, the dynamics of the fracture are resolved under an overdamped dynamics described in \cref{sec:NetworkDynamics} where $\bm{v}_f=\bm{0}$ in our quasi-static regime while resolving the fracture. The resultant equation of motion of a node $i$ is therefore 
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\bm{r}}_i = \sum_{j}\mu_{ij} \frac{l_{ij}-l_{ij,0}}{l_{ij,0}}\frac{\bm{r}_{ij}}{\left\lVert \bm{r}_{ij} \right\rVert}, \text{ for all neighbours $j$ of $i$.}
    \label{eq:DoubleMotion}
\end{equation}
The spring modulus $\mu_{ij}$ takes either $\mu_s$ or $\mu_m$, and time units are set by the drag coefficient $\zeta=1$. We integrate \cref{eq:DoubleMotion} with an adaptive Euler-Heun algorithm (described in \cref{sec:EulerHeun}) with an absolute error tolerance of $10^{-2}$. Bond breakages are evaluated at the start of each time step, and the time step $\delta t$ is reset to $10^{-4}$ to account for the discontinuity resulting from the removed bond. The system dynamics evolves in this manner until the root-mean-square (r.m.s.) force at the nodes again falls below $10^{-6}$, where another trial step of size $\Delta\epsilon=10^{-3}$ is attempted. In this manner, we have implemented a quasi-static pure shear protocol, which we prove matches the limit as $\dot{\gamma} \to 0$ in \cref{sec:DoubleRate}.

In all cases, we choose units of stress such that $\mu_s = 1.0$ and the characteristic length of our sacrificial bonds is set by our network structure, but in both cases considered, it is such that the size of our unit cell $L_{x,0} = L_{x,0} = \sqrt{N_s}$. The length scale of our matrix bonds is then set by $M$, the typical number of matrix-bond lengths per sacrificial-bond length. For notational convenience, we will define $M=0$ to denote a system with only a sacrificial network. In line with the experimental literature discussed previously, we will consider cases where the sacrificial network is comparatively sparse ($M>1.0$), stiffer than the matrix ($\mu_m < 1.0$), and more brittle ($\lambda_m > 0.5$). The data we present subsequently is obtained by averaging over $5$ samples, and for each quantity, we report the mean and standard deviation. 

\section{Stress-Strain Response}

This work aims to explore how the simple mesoscopic model described above can be used to explore how the effect of differing length scales affects the fracture and behaviour of double networks, particularly focusing on how stress is redistributed across the networks following a plastic event, as is explored in \cite{tauber_microscopic_2020, tauber_sharing_2021}. The first key result of this work is shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}, where we compare the stress-strain response of a double network material with its sacrificial and matrix components individually for both a Jamming-based double network. We illustrate that the simple model described above, under a quasi-static pure shear protocol, can capture the correct stress-strain response of experimental systems such as those shown in Ref. \cite{gong_why_2010}.  By comparing the stress-strain response of the double network shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}b to the single networks shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}a, we illustrate that it is possible within our model to create a double network which retains most of the toughness of the matrix network while still retaining the stiffness of the sacrificial network, particularly during the early elastic regime of deformation. 

Firstly, we consider the stress-strain response of a single matrix network (blue curve in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}a) or a single sacrificial network (red curve in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}a), where we observe a non-linear elastic stretching response, often found in the network materials \cite{feng_nonlinear_2016,licup_nonlinear_2016,storm_nonlinear_2005}, followed by abrupt brittle or quasi-brittle failure \cite{dussi_athermal_2020, tauber_stretchy_2022} at its failure strain $\epsilon^*_{s/m}$. This response is to be expected and follows the same principle discussed in \cref{sec:Ultradelayed}. Now, considering the response of the double network (solid black curve in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}b), we see a similar feature with an initial non-linear elastic response, again commonly found in elastomers\cite{millereauMechanicsElastomericMolecular2018, ducrot_toughening_2014}, hydrogels\cite{gong_why_2010} and macroscopic composites\cite{kingMacroscaleDoubleNetworks2019, takahashiCreatingStiffTough2018} up until the first sacrificial bond break and an abrupt failure at its failure strain $\epsilon^*_d$. Between these two features, the double network then undergoes an irreversible softening response starting at the first sacrificial failure at a strain $\epsilon \approx \epsilon^*_{s}$. During this region, the sacrificial component (red dashed line in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}b) contributions to the overall response plateau, which can be seen in both our model and experiments \cite{vernereyStatisticalDamageMechanics2018,millereauMechanicsElastomericMolecular2018}, as the sacrificial bonds fail diffusely. The double network then ultimately goes on to break catastrophically through the same macroscopic failure observed in the single networks as the physics becomes dominated by the matrix component (blue dashed line in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}b). In the results that follow, we will explore the underlying physics which drives this difference in response, how stress delocalisation stops macroscopic fracture and how $M$ and $\mu$ can be tuned to create an optimal double network.

To quantify how effective a set of parameters $M$, $\mu_m$ and $\mu_s$ is we characterise each double networks mechanical response by extracting its failure strain $\epsilon^*_d$, its failure stress $\Sigma^*_d$ (defined in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}) and initial modulus $G_s$, $G_m$ and $G_d$ of the sacrificial component, matrix component and combined double network, respectively (defined via the initial stress-strain slopes in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain}). Correspondingly, for each component of every double network, we realise the associated single network and quantify its mechanical response in the same way, obtaining either $\epsilon_s$ and $\Sigma_s$ or $\epsilon_m$ and $\Sigma_m$ depending on the origin of the single network.

As discussed previously, a well-designed tough double network has two key requirements. Together, these requirements serve as the criteria by which we assess the success of our double networks across parameter space:
\begin{enumerate}[I]
    \renewcommand{\theenumi}{\Roman{enumi}}
    \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\theenumi)}
    \item It inherits the toughness of soft and ductile matrix components, with a failure strain $\epsilon^*_D \approx \epsilon^*_m \gg \epsilon^*_s$.
    \item It inherits the stiffness of its stiff but brittle sacrificial component, with modulus $G_d \approx G_s \gg G_m$.
\end{enumerate}
We will address these two requirements in turn. We first focus on property I), examining how the failure strain of the double network changes under $M$, $\mu_m$ and $\lambda_m$.

We illustrate property I) in \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}a-b and \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagrams}a where we show how the failure strain $\epsilon^*$ changes as a function of our control parameters $M$, $\mu_m$ and $\lambda_m$. In \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagrams}a we plot a phase space diagram of $M$ against $\mu_m$ for $\lambda_m=3.0$, showing an increased failure toughness as either increasing $M$ or $\mu_m$. This trend is shown more clearly in the upper solid line of \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}a, which represents a horizontal slice of \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagrams}a at $\mu_m=0.2$. For all values of $\lambda_m$ in \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}a we observe a constant pattern: as $M \to \infty$ the network's toughness transitions from being close to the sacrificial failure point $\epsilon^*_d \approx \epsilon^*_s$ towards the toughness of the matrix, with $\epsilon_d^* \to \epsilon_m^*$. We do note a less pronounced upwards trend for $\lambda_m=1.0$ where the matrix and sacrificial bonds have similar breakage thresholds; in this case, early failures in the sacrificial networks propagate enough stress to result in premature failures in the matrix. In contrast, the curves corresponding to $\lambda_m=2.0$ or $3.0$ show that allowing enough separation in the failure of the two bond types ensures that the failure requires a significant weakening in the sacrificial network before it fails. We will return to this behaviour in more detail later on when we discuss the role of stress propagation.

Property II) is considered in \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagrams}b, where we show the evolution of the relative stiffness contribution of the sacrificial component, quantified by the ratio $G_s/G_d$. As either $M$ or $\mu_m$ increase, we see a decrease in $G_s/G_d$, indicating an increasing contribution of the matrix network to the overall stiffness $G_d$. This behaviour is expected and indicates the trivial limit at $M \to \infty$ or $\mu_m \to \infty$: the double network effectively just becomes a single matrix network, inheriting its softness. While this trivial limit does therefore formally satisfy property II), it does so at the expense of losing any contribution from the sacrificial network and is therefore unable to satisfy property I).

This trend is clearly shown in  \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract}e-d where the decrease in $G_s/G_d$ is more apparent. We do not plot the values relative to $\lambda_m$ since the reported moduli are taken as the initial slope of the stress-strain response, where no bonds have failed. As a result, this is independent of $\lambda_m$, $\lambda_s$, and we are unable to report the single network moduli in this context. 

Considering the trends shown in \cref{fig:JammedPhaseDiagrams} and \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract} in the context of the desired double network properties I) and II), it becomes clear that achieving the desired optimal double network properties requires a trade-off. Optimising property I), for a given $\lambda_m$, drives towards increasing $M$ and $\mu_m$ until the double network material fails like the single matrix network (whose failure point $\epsilon^*_m$ only depends on $\lambda_m$). At this point, the sacrificial network contributes very little, and the single matrix network should be used due to its easier synthesis. 

Conversely, decreasing $M$ or $\mu_m$ enhances the contribution of the sacrificial network to the overall stiffness but drives the material's failure strain towards that of the sacrificial network. An optimal set of parameters, therefore, requires a compromise: for a given $\lambda_m$ whose value is sufficiently large to stop an immediate failure of the matrix, a pair of parameters $M$, $\mu_m$ should be chosen such that the failure strain $\epsilon^*_d \approx \epsilon^*_m$ is large, while the sacrificial network still dominates $G_d$. In practice, we found that the region of parameter space exemplified by the values $M = 3.0$, $\mu_m = 0.2$, $\lambda_m = 3.0$ shown in \cref{fig:JammedStressStrain} to be a sensible compromise.

\section{Stress Propagation} \label{sec:doubleStressProp}

To understand the trends described above, and in particular, the enhanced toughness of the double networks, we first consider the effect of single bond breaking in a single network material. When such a network is deformed along the $y$ axis until a single bond fails, the failure of a single bond forces the non-affine local rearrangement of surrounding nodes as the system tries to relax to its new minimum energy state. As the network does this, it redistributes some of the stress which was contained in the now broken bond. This results in an increase in the local stresses of bonds aligned along the $x$ axis (and a decrease in bonds along the $y$ axis) as seen in the left column of \cref{fig:JammedPropogators}. Unless otherwise stopped, these neighbouring bonds may then also break, or are more likely to break after continued deformation, further propagating stress to their neighbours. 

Unless otherwise stopped, this stress redistribution makes these neighbouring bonds more susceptible to failure. This could result in these bonds failing during the continued deformation of the network, or more importantly, being immediately taken above their threshold. In either case, these subsequent breakages result in further rearrangements and increased stress on neighbouring bonds. This process will ultimately, unless stopped, result in an avalanche that propagates macroscopically along the $x$ axis, ultimately resulting in catastrophic material failure, as illustrated in \cref{fig:JammedSacrifical}.

Instead, in a double network where we have a matrix network, when a sacrificial bond is broken, some of its stress is absorbed through the matrix, which, assuming $\lambda_m$ is large enough, is tough enough to resist failure. Consequently, the amount of stress the neighbouring sacrificial bonds have to absorb is reduced, leading them to be less prone to failing. 

Optimally, to satisfy properties I) and II) discussed earlier, the matrix must be sufficiently stiff and tough to absorb enough stress to arrest the chain of sacrificial bond failures, while remaining compliant enough that it does not dominate the initial modulus $G_d$. This reduction in local stresses on neighbouring sacrificial bonds allows for other sacrificial bonds elsewhere in the material to fail instead, resulting in ductile failure of the sacrificial component.

\section{Conclusion}
In this chapter, we studied both the macroscopic and microscopic fracture behaviour of double networks in a simple mesoscopic model. The central aim of this work was to further understand how stress delocalisation in the sacrificial component of the double networks allows for the double network to inherit both the stiffness of its stiff and brittle sacrificial component and the ductility of its soft and ductile matrix component. We demonstrate that it is possible to use a simple model to capture the complex physics of double network materials, and show by numerical simulation that these results capture the key qualitative features shown in experiments. 

The framework has allowed us to systematically explore the role of stiffness and network length scales in controlling the response of double network materials. In doing so, we reinforce the ideas of previous experimental and theoretical results showing increased ductility of double networks arises from a reduction in the Eshelby stress propagator between neighbouring sacrificial bonds. In particular, we highlight that the increased ductility of double networks arises from a reduction in the Eshelby stress propagator between neighbouring sacrificial bonds. This reduction suppresses the cascade of bond failures that would otherwise lead to brittle fracture in a single sacrificial network, thereby promoting diffuse damage and delayed macroscopic failure. 

The model we present here is clearly a simplification when compared to real double network hydrogel systems. In this work, we have only restricted our analysis to the case of two-dimensional systems in a central force model, without any bending modulus $\kappa$. Clearly, this model is unable to fully capture real experimental systems; therefore, further work may wish to consider incorporating the following physics.

For the bulk of this work, we have considered networks above the isostatic point with an average network coordination $z \geq 4=2d$ and with no pre-stress (a brief exploration can be seen in \cref{sec:SquareAppendix}). These assumptions simplify the physics relative to experimental systems, which have connectivity well below the isostatic point of $z=6=2d$. Extending the model to either three dimensions or to sub-isostatic connectivities should allow for more physical simulations.

In combination, the sacrificial network is often synthesised first in an experimental system, resulting in it being naturally prestressed in the double network's natural condition. In our current construction, we do not consider this apart from the constraint that $\mu_s > \mu_m$. Incorporating controlled pre-stress into the sacrificial network would be straightforward within our framework, following the approach outlined for square double networks in \cref{sec:SquareAppendix}. Further adding disorder in this pre-stress may allow for more complex behaviour as the components transition from pro to sub-isostatic during fracture.

These two alternative network geometries could be further extended by alternative potentials such as a rope-like potential \cite{arzashStressstabilizedSubisostaticFiber2019a}, generating more physically accurate network geometries using more complex molecular dynamics simulations \cite{mugnaiInterspeciesInteractionsDual2025,rijns_synthetic_2024}.  

Alternatively, further big questions in the double network literature could be considered. In our framework, we have considered fracture to be irreversible, stopping the formation of new bonds during deformation. However, a growing body of experimental work on double network hydrogels has demonstrated that incorporating self-healing through the reformation of bonds as an alternative route to toughness and durability\cite{wangRapidSelfstrengtheningDoublenetwork2025,yasuiRateIndependentSelfHealingDouble2022,yinSelfHealingHydrogelsSynthesis2023}. This has been particularly shown the the context of cyclic shear loading where experimental works \cite{zhang_fatigue_2018, chen_improvement_2016, chen_novel_2015} have shown that, double-network hydrogels can endure many loading cycles with little loss of stiffness. This work shows that sacrificial bonds in the first network repeatedly break while the second network continues to carry the load. 

This result follows very closely with the results shown in our work a one of the many driving contributors to tough networks in shear start-up, therefore a thorough exploration of our model, which should be simple enough to explore the \quotes{timescales} necessary, may be able to provide a deep insight into these results. Further, recent experimental work done by \quotes{you et al}\cite{you_damage_2025} has been able to establish a simple connection between monotonic and cyclic loading behaviours through damage modelling, which we may be able to explore more extensively. 

In summary, this simplified approach has enabled us to isolate the key physical mechanisms governing fracture in double networks using minimal ingredients. We hope that this framework will serve as a foundation for future studies addressing more complex and realistic systems.

\section{Square Lattice-Based Network} \label{sec:SquareAppendix}
The simplest form of a single network structure is that of a lattice-based network. We extend this trivially to a double network by constructing commensurate lattices with different length scales connected where lattice sites overlap. We hope that, under this alternative method of network construction, to reinforce the ideas presented in the main text and confirm that the physics we observe holds more generally. 

\subsection{Lattice-Based Double Networks}
 For our lattice-based double network, the sacrificial network is constructed using the same method described in \cref{sec:LatticeBasedNetworks} by defining a set of lattice vectors and rules for connecting them. The matrix network can also be generated in the same way, however, with a scaled set of lattice vectors. For simplicity, we choose $M\in\mathbb{Z}$, such that the sacrificial and matrix lattices are commensurate: the matrix lattice vectors are integer multiples of the sacrificial ones. This ensures that the nodes of the matrix lattice lie exactly on a subset of the nodes of the fine sacrificial lattice. The natural length of each bond $l_0$ is then set in this configuration.  

While we choose to ignore the volume exclusion principle during the dynamics of the simulation to simplify the forces in our system, only to consider central forces, we can optionally decide to respect this in the network generation steps. This gives us the choice of whether we allow both a matrix and a sacrificial bond to perfectly lie on top of each other in the networks during the network's natural configuration. It's also possible to add further to subdivide the sacrificial bonds such that they connect at every lattice point of the matrix they pass over. While this would normally give four combinations of lattice-based network geometries, the case where we do not allow overlaps and do not subdivide the sacrificial bonds decouples the matrix and sacrificial networks, and therefore cannot be considered a double network. An example of the three remaining cases can be seen in \cref{fig:DoubleSquareTypes} for a square lattice with $M=4$.

\section{Dependency on matrix threshold} \label{sec:JammedlambdaDependecy}

In \cref{fig:JammedStrainExtract,fig:JammedBrokenExtract} we briefly explore the effect of $\lambda_m$ by plotting the behaviour at distinct values $\lambda_m = 1.0,\; 2.0,\; 3.0$. We extend this analysis by plotting intermediate values of $\lambda_m$ for the case of a double network with parameters $M=3$, $\mu_m=0.2$, $\lambda_s=0.5$ for characteristic quantities $\epsilon^*$ and $B^*$. 

In \cref{fig:DoubleLambda}a we plot the failure strain $\epsilon^*$, which show the expected increase with $\lambda_m$ for both the single matrix networks and double networks. For the single matrix networks, $\epsilon^*$ increase linearly with $\lambda_m$. However with an increase in variance across samples, we attribute this the the growing magnitude of non-affine rearrangements which allows for matrix bonds to break earlier or later than a linear model would predict. The double networks exhibit a similar, approximately linear relation dependence on $\lambda_m$ for $\lambda_m \gtrsim 1.5$, where we do not observe any early matrix failures. 

In \cref{fig:DoubleLambda}b-c, we plot the analogue of \cref{fig:JammedBrokenExtract}. Specifically \cref{fig:DoubleLambda}b indicates the number of failed bonds at $\epsilon^*$ in the single sacrificial (red) and single matrix (blue) networks. In both case, we observe little or no dependence on $\lambda_m$, either trivially for the sacrificial network or due to abrupt brittle failures found in the matrix network discussed in the main text.

The double network material exhibits a non-monotonic response for $\lambda_m < 1.5$, which can be understood as follows. for $\lambda_m \approx \lambda_s=0.5$ bond breakages occur uniformly across the components and each undergo the slightly ductile response as shown in sacrificial network in \cref{fig:JammedBrokenStrain}a and therefore $\epsilon_d^* \approx \epsilon_s^*$. At $\lambda_m=1.0$ only the sacrificial network breaks diffusely. However, the stress propagated by the broken sacrificial bonds is sufficient to fracture the matrix once enough spatially local damage has accumulated, leading to premature matrix failure.
